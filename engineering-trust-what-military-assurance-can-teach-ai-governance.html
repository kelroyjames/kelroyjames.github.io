Engineering Trust: Assurance When the Human Leaves the Point of Work

What happens to assurance when there is no longer a person standing at the point of work? Naval logistics assurance and the emerging debate on AI governance are converging on that same question, from opposite directions.

Kelroy James Updated September 2026
📖 13 min read 📊 2,800 words
Share on LinkedIn (opens in a new tab) Share on X (opens in a new tab) Share via Email

What happens to assurance when there is no longer a person standing at the point of work? That sounds like a question about artificial intelligence, but it is also a question I recognise from naval logistics. For most of my career, assurance has rested on a simple assumption: someone is responsible for the thing being controlled. Someone holds the account, exercises judgement over what they see, and can explain what happened if asked. When something goes wrong, that same person can be held accountable for it. Replace the person with an AI agent, an autonomous system or an uncrewed platform, and that assumption becomes much harder to sustain.

I first explored this territory in a presentation to the Global Skill Development Council on 19 February 2026, mapping naval logistics assurance practice against ISO/IEC 42001, the international standard for AI management systems. Reading recent work from Deloitte and Boston Consulting Group on AI-enabled assurance and agentic systems made the comparison difficult to ignore. Both fields are confronting the same underlying problem from opposite directions: how to maintain trust and accountability when authority, activity and decision-making move away from the person who has traditionally stood at the point of work. The industries could hardly be more different. The assurance problem is not.

The point of work matters

One of the most useful ideas in AI-enabled assurance is that assurance should happen as close as possible to where the work takes place. Deloitte describes this as assurance generated at source, built into how a process operates, rather than checked only after the event. That principle is already familiar in naval logistics. Routine checks on stores and equipment accounts sit with the most junior people in the department, because carrying out the check is how they learn to own the risk. The officer responsible for the department then applies judgement on top of that: does what they are seeing make sense, and does the account reflect what is actually happening on the ship?

"The long-standing ambition of assurance by design becomes practically achievable at scale."Deloitte, September 2026

When the answer is yes, a short entry confirming that nothing significant was found can be a complete record. There is no value in producing several pages of paperwork simply to prove a negative. The record's value comes from the competence and judgement behind it rather than its length. A short entry can carry more weight than a long checklist when the person making the judgement is close to the risk, understands what they are looking at, and can explain their decision if challenged. The same principle applies directly to AI governance, where the quality of a control matters more than the volume of paperwork built up around it.

When inspection needs to mean something

There is a second lesson from logistics assurance: inspection does not have to be advisory. In naval logistics, an independent inspection can produce a graded outcome, and a department that fails outright must rectify the problem and undergo re-inspection before anything proceeds. The result carries real consequences rather than sitting as a recommendation on a report. That matters directly for AI governance. Suppose an AI system fails a material safety, fairness or control test: an assurance function can document the finding and make a recommendation, but if that recommendation has no ability to affect whether the system keeps running, the assurance mechanism is limited to reporting the problem rather than stopping it.

The stronger model gives assurance enough authority to change the operational outcome, beyond simply describing it. There is, however, a real limitation: inspection works best when there is an identifiable unit to inspect, a department, a site, an account, something with a defined boundary and somebody answerable for it. Activity that happens below that boundary becomes difficult to see. This is particularly relevant to agentic AI, where an agent can operate across applications, accounts and workflows without fitting neatly inside the organisational unit an existing inspection regime recognises. The inspection model itself has probably not failed here. The boundary around what is being inspected may simply no longer reflect where the activity takes place.

The handover question

AI governance frameworks place considerable emphasis on identifying who owns an AI system and who is accountable for it, and that emphasis is necessary. ISO/IEC 42001's provisions on AI roles and responsibilities are a good example of that emphasis in practice: the standard requires organisations to name an accountable owner for every AI system and to have a backup and escalation route agreed in advance, which is a real and useful requirement. What the standard leaves open is whether to run a fresh audit of the system's risk position at the point accountability actually transfers, rather than requiring it outright. A separate question matters just as much: what happens, in practice, when ownership actually changes hands?

Naval logistics assurance answers that question directly. When responsibility for an account passes to a new officer, the incoming custodian can be required to accept the account only after it has been audited and any findings recorded. In naval logistics, a change of responsibility is never simply an administrative event. What matters here is the underlying idea rather than the specific military procedure: a change in accountability is a natural trigger to reassess risk, and that principle sits comfortably alongside the named-owner model ISO/IEC 42001 already requires.

That has an obvious application to AI governance. A change of owner does not need to mean only an updated register and a handover email. It can be an opportunity to re-baseline the risk position properly. The issue becomes even more important when an AI agent acts under authority originally granted to a human. Boston Consulting Group's Matteo Coppola and Anne Kleppe describe a related problem in their work on agentic banking: an agent may inherit access originally granted to a supervised human, while the resulting audit trail identifies the credential without necessarily identifying the agent, the delegation or the rationale behind the action. That creates a very different kind of handover problem, one where the question is no longer simply who owns the system, but who authorised this specific action, under what delegation, and what evidence shows how that authority was actually exercised.

The assumption underneath

This is where the comparison between logistics assurance and AI governance becomes most useful, because the mechanisms described above all depend on something easy to overlook: there is a person standing at the point of work. The junior rating learns to own the risk because they are physically present with the materiel. The handover audit works because accountability moves from one named human custodian to another. The short record is meaningful because a competent person looked, exercised judgement, and can explain what they saw if asked. Remove the person, and each mechanism starts to lose the thing that made it work in the first place.

That is the problem AI governance is beginning to expose. An AI agent can inherit authority granted to a human, act under that authority, and generate an audit trail, but the trail may identify the credential rather than the agent, the delegation or the reasoning behind the action. That raises a genuinely difficult assurance question: can accountability still be established if the person holding the authority did not directly perform the action, did not directly observe it, and could not necessarily predict what the system would do? Research into autonomous military systems raises a closely related problem. A study published through the NATO Command and Control Centre of Excellence examined accountability when decisions are delegated to autonomous weapon systems, and found responsibility distributed across developers, procurement organisations, commanders, operators and the system itself.

Kjell Schipper and Arnoud Lagendijk of Radboud University Nijmegen, writing in the Centre's Annals of Command and Control, interviewed eleven Royal Netherlands Army stakeholders, legal advisors, operational commanders, AI specialists and policymakers, about how the Army preserves accountability once decisions are delegated to autonomous weapon systems. They found no single actor retaining full situational control at the decisive moment, a condition they term an accountability vacuum. One of their interviewees put the underlying issue plainly: "If you're going to hold somebody accountable, there has to be a level of predictability in that it was going to go wrong. You can't hold somebody accountable for something that they could have in no way known was going to happen." The details differ from banking and logistics, but the underlying problem is familiar in both: responsibility can become distributed faster than accountability mechanisms can follow it. That suggests the issue is bigger than AI governance on its own. It is an assurance problem created by delegated authority itself, and it shows up wherever authority, action and evidence start to separate from the person who used to hold all three at once.

When the human is no longer holding the asset

The logistics version of this problem is particularly tangible. Imagine materiel being held or moved by an uncrewed or automated system, operating under authority delegated to a person who may be nowhere near it. If the asset moves under pre-authorised instructions and is later lost, the question becomes who was actually holding it at the material time. There may be a named account, a system log, even telemetry, but there may be no human custodian who was physically present when the loss occurred, and that distinction matters more than it first appears.

An investigation into a lost asset needs more than a record showing which credential authorised the activity. It needs to establish:

  • what the system was authorised to do
  • what it actually did
  • which agent or process executed the action
  • what authority was delegated
  • what information was available at the time
  • what evidence explains the decision that was made

These are old assurance questions, but AI and autonomous systems are making them newly urgent. That vocabulary of custody, built originally for stores accounts and equipment logs, turns out to be useful well beyond logistics: it gives AI governance another way of thinking about accountability that does not depend on the person who used to be standing there.

Deciding what is worth keeping

AI-enabled assurance is moving towards embedded and preventative controls rather than relying entirely on manual detective controls, and that shift is generally the right one. It does not mean every control should be automated, and it does not mean every event needs the same level of documentation. Naval logistics assurance offers a useful discipline for deciding where assurance effort should actually go: some checks are legally or operationally non-negotiable, while others are discretionary and should be reviewed according to their real level of risk. Not every control needs to move at the same speed, and treating them as though they do wastes effort on the parts that were never the risk in the first place.

For AI governance, that means identifying the controls that are genuinely load-bearing: safety, legal exposure, material bias or discrimination thresholds, security and availability. The controls that protect those outcomes deserve the greatest attention, while others can be monitored at a different frequency or through different forms of evidence. The goal is to concentrate assurance effort where the consequence actually justifies it. That becomes particularly important as AI systems move towards continuous decision-making, where a periodic review model may suit some risks and be completely inadequate for others. The answer should be set by the consequence of failure and the speed at which the risk can change, which is a far more useful test than simply asking whether a control happens to be automated or manual.

The claim about speed here is a narrow one. Corporate compliance tooling has already moved past monthly cycles for anything genuinely high-risk. Continuous controls monitoring platforms now check critical items hourly or daily, and the UK Corporate Governance Code's newest provision expects boards to back control effectiveness with real-time evidence rather than periodic snapshots. The principle naval logistics assurance shares with that shift is risk-based frequency, not the exact interval. Coppola and Kleppe make the same point about frequency more bluntly, from inside a bank: validation built for periodic, human-paced review does not hold once a system is composing decisions continuously across a portfolio.

Four questions for an AI governance programme

If the argument above holds, an AI governance programme should be able to answer four practical questions, and they are assurance questions rather than purely technical ones.

  • Who is the named, accountable owner of each AI system, and does a change of owner trigger a fresh look at its risk position?
  • Which controls are genuinely load-bearing, and which persist largely because they have always existed?
  • When an AI agent acts under inherited authority, does the record identify the agent, the delegation and the rationale, or only the credential?
  • Which parts of AI activity sit below the organisational unit that current inspection or audit arrangements recognise?

What AI governance can learn from the comparison

I do not think military assurance provides a ready-made model for AI governance. The environments are too different, and the consequences of failure are different too. The value of the comparison is that it exposes an assumption that can otherwise stay hidden: traditional assurance has been built around identifiable custody, where someone owns the asset, someone controls the process, someone exercises judgement, and someone can be asked what happened. AI and autonomous systems are beginning to separate those things from each other. The person who owns the system may not be the person who initiated the action, and the person who authorised the system may not be the person who observed the outcome.

The credential can be identifiable even when the agent, the delegation and the rationale behind an action are not, and that is exactly where assurance needs to evolve next. The task now goes beyond monitoring whether an AI system complies with a written policy: it means establishing whether authority, action, evidence and accountability still connect to each other at all, in the way they always did when a named person stood at the point of work. It is the harder and more useful question, and the one this comparison was really about from the start.

Where this argument goes next

The comparison is useful precisely because it is not a finished answer. Naval logistics assurance provides mechanisms that remain valuable on their own terms: judgement placed close to the risk, inspection with consequences that actually bite, reassessment when accountability changes hands, and a deliberate distinction between controls that are load-bearing and controls that persist through habit. The same model also exposes its own limit, which is the question this whole piece has been circling: what happens when there is no longer a person standing at the point of work? That question is becoming relevant to AI governance, agentic systems and autonomous operations all at once, and it is becoming relevant to physical logistics at exactly the same time.

The Royal Navy's own pivot towards a Hybrid Navy, a mixed force of crewed and uncrewed platforms, raises the same question directly. Atlantic Bastion and Project BEEHIVE are among the publicly named initiatives behind that pivot. In my own assessment, publicly available material on it has not yet set out a logistics estimate for how custody is verified, who is accountable if the asset is lost or damaged, or what evidential standard the record must meet, once materiel sits with an uncrewed system rather than a person. It is the same gap this essay has traced through banking and AI governance, arriving in physical logistics from its own direction rather than borrowed from either.

I intend to take that question into my dissertation for the Defence Logistics Staff Course, drawing on eighteen years across two assurance disciplines, movements and logistics, both built around a person standing at the point of work, and now being asked to answer for a force that is deliberately removing the person from some of those points on purpose. The question this essay opened with was never really about artificial intelligence alone. The challenge, in the end, goes beyond automating assurance. It means engineering trust once the human point of custody is no longer physically present to vouch for it.

The views expressed here are my own and are based on general observations about logistics assurance and AI governance. They draw only on published sources and do not represent the position of the Royal Navy, the Ministry of Defence or any organisation with which I work. Naval practice is described in general terms.
