The Convergence of Cyber-GRC and Physical Logistics

Why siloed supply chain assurance creates blind spots, where they form, and what happens to custody when nobody is holding the item.

Kelroy James Updated September 2026
📖 10 min read 📊 1,962 words
Share on LinkedIn (opens in a new tab) Share on X (opens in a new tab) Share via Email

What happens when two functions can each report themselves fully compliant, both be telling the truth, and the risk still falls through the gap between them? That sounds like a question about enterprise risk architecture, but it is also one I recognise from eighteen years spent on one side of exactly that gap. Supply chain assurance often rests on a simple assumption: if the cyber dashboard is green and the logistics dashboard is green, the enterprise is safe. That assumption holds until someone with subpoena powers looks at the point where they meet.

In August 2026 the United States Department of State concluded a $36 million settlement with BAE Systems, Inc. It resolved 104 charged violations of the Arms Export Control Act and the International Traffic in Arms Regulations between 19 May 2019 and 24 March 2025. Half the penalty, $18 million, was suspended against remedial compliance measures. The company had also disclosed the violations itself in more than twenty voluntary disclosures, which fits the profile of an organisation that found its own problem. The part worth reading closely is the charging letter's account of how a compliant supply chain team, a compliant IT system and a compliant workforce still produced 104 violations between them.

The letter deals first with a single episode, over 13 and 14 December 2023, in which technical data related to printed wiring boards for GPS receivers reached a manufacturer in the People's Republic of China, a destination proscribed under 22 C.F.R. 126.1. Because each transmitted document was charged separately, that one episode accounts for 38 of the 104 violations. The letter records four conditions behind it: the supply chain team did not fully understand the applicable export control regulations, the secure file transfer network did not adequately flag export control warnings before transmission, no formal training or written process governed use of that network, and there was no adequate understanding of who export control questions should be put to. Those four conditions are worth reading closely, because similar gaps form in ordinary supply chains and may widen once custody moves to systems.

Two green dashboards

From a stores account, the four conditions describe one failure from four directions: the people handling the material did not hold the regulatory knowledge, the system moving the data did not carry the warning, no written process governed the system, and nobody knew who to ask. Each function could have reported itself compliant within its own scope, and each would have been telling the truth as it understood it. The United States Government nonetheless assessed that certain of the unauthorised exports created the potential for harm to national security.

Recent research has turned to how cyber-risk management strategies can be integrated (International Journal of Physical Distribution & Logistics Management, 2024), which implies that the functions involved are still commonly run apart. Cybersecurity and governance teams tend to work on ISO/IEC 27001 scopes, vendor assessments and access control, while trade compliance and logistics teams work on customs declarations, controlled goods, freight contracts and inventory records. The board receives a separate update from each, and both can be green while the enterprise remains exposed to loss, disruption and enforcement. In my view, risk moves along the chain of custody in a way neither function is placed to see as a whole.

"Two green dashboards show only that two functions are each telling the truth about what they can see, and nothing about the point where they meet."

The blind spot

The research on managing cyber risk in supply chains has been reviewed and an agenda set for further work (Ghadge et al., 2020), and published practice now covers cybersecurity supply chain risk management for systems and organisations (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2022). Both treat a supplier's cyber exposure as a supply chain risk, and the consequences can cross from software into transport. A ransomware attack on a tier-two component supplier might leave the buyer's own systems untouched and still stop manufacturing, at which point the cyber incident has become a readiness problem. Run the other way, an inventory ledger may record five hundred critical replacement units in stock while handovers and incomplete records have quietly produced unrecorded damage and scrap, without anybody falsifying anything.

The National Cyber Security Centre's guidance on mapping a supply chain asks organisations to record sub-tier relationships alongside the assurance evidence held for each supplier, which points to the same gap in supplier assurance. A supplier may submit a completed questionnaire and hold valid certifications, and an adversarial review of its sub-tier dependencies, filings and ownership signals may then surface concentration risk and indicators of export control exposure the vendor portal was never designed to look for. In all three cases each function reported accurately on what it was built to see, so the risk sat where nobody was watching.

The picking error

The charging letter contains a second episode that anyone who has run a stores account may recognise. In September 2020 an export compliance officer approved the export of an F404 ECU, an item subject to the Export Administration Regulations, to Switzerland. The shipping department then picked a T700 Full Authority Digital Engine Control from the export hold shelf, an item controlled under USML Category XIX(e) and designated Significant Military Equipment. Dock personnel packed it without verifying its part number against the shipping memorandum. It left under the wrong regulation entirely, cited on the commercial invoice under an Export Control Classification Number instead of its Munitions List category. The compliance decision was correct and made no difference, because the control that should have enforced it was a person checking a part number against a document.

The Export Control Joint Unit's compliance code of practice asks for documented procedures, a named responsible person at director level, training matched to its audience and an adequate audit trail. Almost all of the evidence needed to satisfy it is likely to be created by logistics, procurement and engineering staff who do not think of themselves as operating controls. The picking error suggests that an export control violation can follow from an ordinary stores verification step, one performed thousands of times a day in warehouses everywhere, by people who are rarely told they sit inside a regulatory control environment.

Careful with the word "unified"

What this essay calls unified supply chain governance, my own shorthand and not an established category, means treating physical custody, trade compliance and digital security as connected parts of one control environment. The word needs care, because some separations are controls. Export control judgement is often held apart from commercial pressure so that a shipment deadline cannot lean on a classification decision. The argument here is for visibility across the interfaces, since a single merged dashboard under one owner would be a larger version of the same problem.

When there is nothing to muster

ISO/IEC 27001 builds logging, access review and control testing into information security management. Physical logistics would need the equivalent: rolling physical verification that tests the ledger against what is there. Rolling verification carries two assumptions, however: that there is an item to examine, and a competent person able to go and examine it.

An international standard now sets out how an organisation should manage its AI systems (ISO/IEC 42001, 2023), yet the accountability regimes that govern physical materiel assume a named custodian who can be asked what became of an item. Automated warehousing, uncrewed vehicles and autonomous systems are removing that custodian. Four conditions follow that conventional accountability has no state for. Self-relocating property moves under pre-authorised instructions with no human decision at that moment, so a loss investigation has no custodian to question. Evidence without witnesses leaves machine telemetry as the only record that an asset was destroyed, when it may have been stolen or diverted, and that data is usually assured for operational use and rarely for audit. Adversarial possession puts a spoofed or diverted asset in someone else's custody, often with no ledger state or reporting route for the payload it carried. Migration of value moves the expensive, controlled item between cheap, expendable platforms, so a write-off recorded against the platform records the wrong thing.

Information security already offers a model for the companion control this implies, since ISO/IEC 27001 treats the integrity of logs as something to protect and test. Applied to materiel, the practical shift would be from checking the item to checking the record of the item, because for a growing share of materiel the record may be the only witness there is. The wider accountability question, what happens when authority moves from a person to a system, is set out in Engineering Trust.

Reading the gap before it opens

The BAE charging letter is an excavation conducted after the fact, and the same pattern may be legible much earlier, in the requirements and rapid procurement cases an organisation writes when it specifies something urgently. The signal is an asymmetry between specificity and silence. A requirement that describes the platform, payload and delivery timeline in careful detail, and says nothing about who receipts the materiel or who classifies the licensable components, has scoped the capability and left the control environment unscoped.

The aggravating factors in the BAE case included a failure to implement effective corrective actions addressing the root causes of reported violations. That finding points to compressed timelines as a warning sign. Where the interval between authorisation and delivery is shorter than the time needed to obtain a licence, appoint a competent person or license a storage site, something is likely to be waived. Urgent operations sometimes justify a waiver, and the question is whether it is recorded as a decision with an owner and a closure date or absorbed silently into the tempo.

Testing the join

Siloed assurance tends to ask whether each function meets its own requirements. The BAE letter points to a different comparison, between the records the functions keep: whether the inventory record matches the physical asset, whether the supplier record reflects current ownership and sub-tier structure, whether the export classification matches the configuration and the technical data, and whether the shipment record aligns with the licence and the end-user information. Each of those pairs sits between two owners, and the cyber risk assessment adds one more when it covers the logistics systems the operation depends on.

How disagreement is handled matters as much as the comparison itself. When two sources differ, someone has to decide whether the exception is material, and a write-off in particular needs a record assured to an evidential standard as well as an operational one. From stores work, my view is that agreement between records is easy to test, and the harder job is naming who owns the answer when they differ.

Where the functions meet

On this reading, the risk builds where two functions meet, because neither is placed to see it. In the BAE case it ended as 104 charged violations. In ordinary supply chains it may turn up as a readiness gap or an untested ledger, and once custody moves to autonomous systems as a loss with no custodian left to question. Physical verification, adversarial due diligence, export classification and independent inspection already exist, and none of them answers by default for the point where they meet.

Setting eighteen years of stores accounts, handovers and inspections against ISO/IEC 27001 and 42001, and against the supply chain engineering taught through MIT's MicroMasters programme, is what made those handovers visible to me as one problem. That meeting point probably stays nobody's job until an organisation makes it somebody's, and it is likely to widen as more of what an enterprise holds sits with systems. Organisations that treat it as an assurance problem before a regulator does may find it considerably cheaper than the alternative.

The views expressed here are my own and are based on general observations about logistics assurance, procurement, supply chain resilience, trade compliance and governance. They draw only on published sources and do not represent the position of the Royal Navy, the Ministry of Defence or any organisation with which I work. Where autonomous and uncrewed systems are discussed, the argument is made at the level of general pattern and no specific programme, platform or operation is described. Nothing published here reproduces any organisation's internal policy, procedure or assurance output.
