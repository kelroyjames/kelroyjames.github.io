The Convergence of Cyber-GRC and Physical Logistics

Why siloed supply chain assurance creates blind spots, where they form, and what happens to custody when nobody is holding the item.

Kelroy James Updated September 2026
📖 10 min read 📊 1,915 words
Share on LinkedIn (opens in a new tab) Share on X (opens in a new tab) Share via Email

What happens when two functions can each report themselves fully compliant, both be telling the truth, and the risk still falls through the gap between them? That sounds like a question about enterprise risk architecture, but it is also one I recognise from eighteen years spent on one side of exactly that gap. Supply chain assurance tends to rest on a simple assumption: if the cyber dashboard is green and the logistics dashboard is green, the enterprise is safe. Cybersecurity and governance teams answer for one set of risks, trade compliance and logistics teams answer for another, and each can be accurate within its own scope. The assumption holds until someone with subpoena powers looks at the seam between them.

In August 2026 the United States Department of State concluded a $36 million settlement with BAE Systems, Inc. It resolved 104 charged violations of the Arms Export Control Act and the International Traffic in Arms Regulations between 19 May 2019 and 24 March 2025. Half the penalty, $18 million, was suspended against remedial compliance measures, and the company had disclosed the violations itself in more than twenty voluntary disclosures. That is the profile of an organisation that found its own problem. The part worth reading closely is the charging letter's account of how a compliant supply chain team, a compliant IT system and a compliant workforce still produced 104 violations between them.

The letter deals first with a single episode, over 13 and 14 December 2023, in which technical data related to printed wiring boards for GPS receivers reached a manufacturer in the People's Republic of China, a destination proscribed under 22 C.F.R. 126.1. Because each transmitted document was charged separately, that one episode accounts for 38 of the 104 violations. The letter records four conditions behind it: the supply chain team did not fully understand the applicable export control regulations, the secure file transfer network did not adequately flag export control warnings before transmission, no formal training or written process governed use of that network, and there was no adequate understanding of who export control questions should be put to.

Two green dashboards

I recognised the shape of that immediately. The four conditions describe one failure from four directions: the people handling the material did not hold the regulatory knowledge, the system moving the data did not carry the warning, no written process governed the system, and nobody knew who to ask. Each function could have reported itself compliant within its own scope, and each would have been telling the truth as it understood it. The United States Government still assessed that certain of the unauthorised exports created the potential for harm to national security.

That arrangement is ordinary. Cybersecurity and governance teams work on ISO/IEC 27001 scopes, vendor assessments, access control and zero-trust architecture. Trade compliance and logistics teams, often on a different site, work on customs declarations, controlled goods, hazardous cargo documentation, freight contracts and inventory records. The board receives a separate update from each, and both are green. The enterprise can still be exposed to inventory loss, operational disruption and enforcement, because risk moves along the chain of custody and neither function can see the whole of it.

"Two green dashboards prove only that two functions are each telling the truth about what they can see, and nothing about the seam between them."

The blind spot

A modern supply chain is a socio-technical system, and a failure rarely stays inside either software or transport. A tier-two component supplier suffers a ransomware attack. The buyer's own systems are untouched, but manufacturing stops, a maintenance schedule slips and contractual consequences follow, so the cyber incident has become a readiness problem. Run it the other way and an inventory ledger records five hundred critical replacement units in stock while handovers, transport conditions and incomplete records have quietly produced damage and unrecorded scrap. Nobody falsified anything. The ledger was simply never tested against the shelf.

Supplier assurance shows the same gap. A supplier submits a completed questionnaire and holds valid certifications, and an adversarial review of its sub-tier dependencies, filings and ownership signals then surfaces concentration risk and indicators of export control exposure the vendor portal was never designed to look for. The questionnaire was answered honestly and the certificate is real. In all three cases each function reported accurately on what it was built to see, and nobody was watching the seam.

The picking error

The charging letter contains a second episode that anyone who has run a stores account should read. In September 2020 an export compliance officer approved the export of an F404 ECU, an item subject to the Export Administration Regulations, to Switzerland. The shipping department then picked a T700 Full Authority Digital Engine Control from the export hold shelf, an item controlled under USML Category XIX(e) and designated Significant Military Equipment. Dock personnel packed it without verifying its part number against the shipping memorandum, and it left under the wrong regulation entirely, cited on the commercial invoice under an Export Control Classification Number instead of its Munitions List category. The compliance decision was correct and made no difference, because the control that should have enforced it was a person checking a part number against a document.

That is an export control violation caused by an ordinary stores verification step, one performed thousands of times a day in warehouses everywhere, by people who are rarely told they sit inside a regulatory control environment. The Export Control Joint Unit's compliance code of practice asks for documented procedures, a named responsible person at director level, training matched to its audience and an adequate audit trail. Almost all of the evidence needed to satisfy it is created by logistics, procurement and engineering staff who do not think of themselves as operating controls.

Careful with the word "unified"

Bridging the divide means treating physical custody, trade compliance and digital security as connected parts of one control environment, which I describe as unified supply chain governance. That is my own shorthand, not an established category, and the word needs care, because some separations are controls. Export control judgement is often held apart from commercial pressure precisely so that a shipment deadline cannot lean on a classification decision, and segregation of duties is a deliberate silo. What I am arguing for is visibility across the interfaces and a board view that shows the dependencies between them. A single merged dashboard under one owner would be a larger version of the same problem.

When there is nothing to muster

Information security tests its logs, access reviews and controls. Physical logistics needs the equivalent: rolling physical verification that tests the ledger against what is actually there. The National Cyber Security Centre's guidance on mapping a supply chain makes the same point about suppliers, recommending ongoing oversight of critical suppliers and their sub-tier relationships over a single assessment. Rolling verification carries two assumptions, however: that there is an item to examine, and a competent person able to go and examine it.

Automated warehousing, uncrewed vehicles, remotely operated plant and autonomous maritime and aerial systems are removing both. Four conditions follow that conventional accountability has no state for. Self-relocating property moves under pre-authorised instructions with no human decision at that moment, so a loss investigation has no custodian to question. Evidence without witnesses leaves machine telemetry as the only record that an asset was destroyed and not stolen or diverted, and that data is usually assured for operational use, not for an audit. Adversarial possession puts a spoofed or diverted asset in someone else's custody, often with no ledger state or reporting route for the payload it carried. Migration of value moves the expensive, controlled item between cheap, expendable platforms, so a write-off recorded against the platform records the wrong thing.

Rolling verification cannot reach any of these, since there is nothing to muster and nobody who saw. It needs a companion control that tests the integrity of the machine record, the same discipline information security already applies to its logs. I set out the wider accountability question, what happens when authority moves from a person to a system, in Engineering Trust. For supply chain assurance the practical shift is from checking the item to checking the record of the item, because for a growing share of materiel the record is the only witness there is.

Reading the gap before it opens

Everything above comes from an enforcement outcome, an excavation conducted after the fact. The same pattern is legible much earlier, in the documents an organisation writes when it specifies something urgently: capability requirements, scoping papers and rapid procurement cases. The signal is an asymmetry between specificity and silence. A requirement may describe the platform, payload, operating environment and delivery timeline in careful detail. If it says nothing about who receipts the materiel, who classifies the licensable components or where controlled items will be stored, it has scoped the capability and left the control environment unscoped. The interfaces it leaves unowned are the ones that later appear in a charging letter.

Compressed timelines are the tell. Where the interval between authorisation and delivery is shorter than the time needed to obtain a licence, appoint a competent person or license a storage site, something will be waived. Urgent operations sometimes justify a waiver, and the question is whether it is recorded as a decision with an owner and a closure date or absorbed silently into the tempo. The aggravating factors in the BAE case included a failure to implement effective corrective actions addressing the root causes of reported violations. A finding that is recorded and never closed is only a record of a control that did not work.

The test

Siloed assurance asks whether each function meets its own requirements. That question is necessary, and the more revealing one is whether the interfaces between functions are controlled. When I review a logistics or supply chain control environment, I ask:

  • Whether the inventory record matches the physical asset, and how that is tested
  • Whether the supplier record reflects current ownership and sub-tier structure
  • Whether the export classification matches the configuration and the technical data
  • Whether the shipment record aligns with the licence and the end-user information
  • Whether the cyber risk assessment includes the logistics systems the operation depends on
  • What happens when two sources disagree, and who decides whether an exception is material
  • Whether the record a write-off would rest on is assured to an evidential standard, or only an operational one

The seam is somebody's job

None of this asks an organisation to invent a new control. Physical verification, adversarial due diligence, export classification and independent inspection already exist, usually run well by people who know their own function. What none of them does by default is answer for the seam between them, the interface where a cyber incident becomes a readiness problem, a picking error becomes an export violation, or a lost asset has no custodian left to question.

That interface stays nobody's job until an organisation makes it somebody's, and the BAE letter shows what happens when nobody has. Setting eighteen years of stores accounts, handovers and inspections against ISO/IEC 27001 and 42001 and the supply chain engineering taught through MIT's MicroMasters programme is what made that seam visible to me as one problem. As more of what an enterprise holds sits with systems, the seam widens, and organisations that treat it as an assurance problem before a regulator does will find it considerably cheaper than the alternative.

The views expressed here are my own and are based on general observations about logistics assurance, procurement, supply chain resilience, trade compliance and governance. They draw only on published sources and do not represent the position of the Royal Navy, the Ministry of Defence or any organisation with which I work. Where autonomous and uncrewed systems are discussed, the argument is made at the level of general pattern and no specific programme, platform or operation is described. Nothing published here reproduces any organisation's internal policy, procedure or assurance output.
