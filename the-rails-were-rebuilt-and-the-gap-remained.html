The Rails Were Rebuilt, but the Gap Remained: What Trade Finance Taught Me About Assurance

In 2023 I argued that decentralised finance could help close a gap in trade finance. The rails have since been rebuilt and the gap hasn't moved, which taught me the real problem was never the rails.

Kelroy James Updated September 2026
📖 9 min read 📊 1,726 words
Share on LinkedIn (opens in a new tab) Share on X (opens in a new tab) Share via Email

Can rebuilt payment infrastructure close the trade-finance gap for smaller firms? The question arose when the building society that held my mortgage in Saint Vincent lost its correspondent-banking relationship. With it went the route by which money could reach the account from abroad, although nothing about the account itself had changed. An article I co-authored with Brian Canup for Trade Finance Global (James and Canup, 2023) argued that paper-based, intermediary-heavy trade finance was leaving smaller firms behind, citing an estimated $5 trillion shortfall in financing for SMEs, and that decentralised finance could open access to them. Three years of subsequent evidence now allow that argument to be tested.

The Asian Development Bank (2026) measures the trade-finance gap as the difference between the trade finance firms seek and the finance banks provide, and currently estimates it at $2.5 trillion. The Financial Conduct Authority (2023) uses de-risking to describe banks restricting or ending whole categories of relationship instead of managing each counterparty individually. The problem this essay examines lies between those two definitions: whether faster, more digital rails address the reasons finance is withheld, or only the way it moves once granted. The discussion first considers documentary reform, then settlement technology and the economics of de-risking, before examining the assurance work that intermediaries perform and what the trade-finance data can and cannot show. Each of these has at some point been offered as a route to wider access, which is why each is tested here.

Right about the friction

The World Trade Organization (2016) documented gaps in trade-finance provision that fall most heavily on small and medium-sized enterprises. Legal reform has since addressed part of the documentary burden behind them. The UK's Electronic Trade Documents Act 2023 gave qualifying electronic trade documents the same legal effect as their paper equivalents, building on UNCITRAL's (2017) Model Law on Electronic Transferable Records. In doing so, it resolved the long-standing difficulty that an electronic record could not be "possessed" in the way a paper bill of lading could. Yet the Asian Development Bank (2026) reports that the global gap has remained at $2.5 trillion since 2023. The loss of my own mortgage provider's correspondent relationship fits that pattern, since the constraint appears to have been the correspondent's confidence in the respondent bank, and faster documents would not necessarily have restored it. This suggests that reducing documentary friction, however valuable, may not on its own widen access for the smaller firms the gap affects most.

The problem beneath the payment

Swift (2026) reports that its blockchain-based ledger went live for initial use in July 2026, with seventeen banks across six continents preparing to pilot tokenised cross-border payments on it, and J.P. Morgan's Kinexys business has taken deposit tokens into live institutional use through JPM Coin. Both are the providers' own announcements, however, and they describe new capability, not evidence of wider access. These developments appear to change how value moves between institutions that already deal with one another. The literature on correspondent banking, by contrast, points to a different constraint. The International Monetary Fund (2016) presented the withdrawal of correspondent relationships as a case for policy action, the Financial Stability Board (2017) tracked the decline through its correspondent banking data, and the US Treasury (2023) published a dedicated de-risking strategy. None of these sources appears to locate the problem in settlement speed. For a building society in Saint Vincent, a faster rail would still have required a correspondent willing to accept the relationship in the first place. It may therefore follow that settlement innovation improves execution while leaving the decision to extend access where it was.

De Koker and Casanovas (2024) ask whether de-risking and de-banking amount to an over-compliance dilemma, a framing that resists two easy explanations: that regulation alone causes withdrawal, and that every withdrawal reflects poor judgement. A correspondent bank can be expected to weigh financial-crime exposure, sanctions risk, the quality of the respondent's controls, the reliability of its supervision and the commercial return on offer. The cost of reaching a defensible view on those questions is likely to be largely fixed per relationship, whereas the revenue tends to scale with the relationship's size. The Caribbean recurs as a case throughout this literature, to the extent that the Center for Strategic and International Studies has asked whether de-risking there has become a "new normal". Saint Vincent sits within that pattern, and one reading of the evidence is therefore that small respondent banks may lose access where the cost of confidence exceeds the value of the relationship, a calculation no rail changes.

The work that did not travel

An incoming custodian in naval logistics accepts responsibility for a stores account only after it has been audited, because signing for another person's record is an act of reliance that needs evidence behind it. Correspondent banking appears to rest on a similar logic at a larger scale. The International Finance Corporation (2019) publishes good practice on anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing risk management for emerging-market banks, whose controls their correspondents largely rely on without observing them directly. Tokenisation and shared ledgers, by contrast, are often evaluated by what they remove, whether a reconciliation step, a paper original or an intermediary. Intermediaries, however, appear to perform assurance work as well, since they identify parties, examine documents, monitor transactions and keep the evidence behind each decision. It is suggested, therefore, that removing an intermediary relocates the assurance work it performed, and that the new owner of that risk is rarely named when the move happens.

"Removing an intermediary relocates the assurance work it was doing. The new owner of that risk is rarely named when the move happens."

Unchanged since 2023

The Asian Development Bank's ninth Global Trade Finance Gap Survey (2026), drawing on more than 110 trade-finance providers between 2023 and 2025, put the gap at $2.5 trillion, unchanged in absolute terms since 2023 and down only from 10.6% to roughly 10% of global trade. Its January 2026 release reported rejection rates of 41% for SMEs and 40% for large and mid-cap corporates, the first time the two have sat so close together. Capital constraints, pricing, country risk and transaction size are also likely to influence those figures, so better verification alone could not plausibly close a gap of that scale. Large counterparties, with established banking histories and professional compliance functions, tend to absorb the continuing cost of being understood more easily than smaller ones, whose ownership and trading behaviour may need reassessing long after onboarding. The evidence therefore supports a narrower claim: that some viable trade goes unfinanced because establishing confidence in the counterparty costs more than the relationship is worth.

The whole argument in miniature

On a naval stores account, what a supplier says about itself arrives on paper, and what it actually delivered is tested at the goods-in check, where the two do not always agree. A risk programme built only on the first carries a gap it cannot see, and trade finance appears to face the same distinction between assertion and evidence. UNCITRAL's (2017) model law and the Electronic Trade Documents Act 2023 show where technology earns its place: electronic records can strengthen the provenance of evidence once their integrity and transfer can be demonstrated, and shared ledgers can reduce discrepancies where participants rely on a common record. The Financial Conduct Authority's (2023) concern with blanket de-risking, however, points to the harder requirement, which is assessment of each counterparty on its merits. This suggests that the most useful tools are those that lower the cost of producing, validating and reusing evidence about a counterparty, including public-source verification, and that tools which only accelerate payments between parties who already trust each other contribute least to closing the gap.

Worth less than it looks

Entries on a naval stores account trace back to a named signatory, and a record tends to be only as defensible as the person who signed for it. The US Treasury's (2023) de-risking strategy and the Financial Conduct Authority's (2023) research both direct attention to the equivalent question in banking, namely how institutions reach decisions about individual relationships. Automation can gather evidence, flag inconsistencies and calculate a risk indicator, yet the institution would still need to know who may accept a relationship, on what conditions and when that acceptance should be revisited. Where one institution cannot rely on another's verification, digitalisation may simply reproduce the same checks faster, leaving the duplication across the system in place. It may follow that an automated assessment without clear authority behind it becomes more consistent without becoming more defensible, and that a dashboard delivering consistency alone is worth less than it looks.

An afternoon of honest diagnosis

The questions below can be answered largely from an institution's own files, in an afternoon of honest review, before any new settlement architecture is considered.

  • Which financing or correspondent decisions are limited by settlement infrastructure, and which by the cost of knowing the counterparty?
  • What evidence about a counterparty could be obtained more cheaply than it is now, and who would need to accept it?
  • Where does the institution rely on self-attestation, and what would independent, public-source verification add?
  • Can an assessment already made be reused elsewhere in the institution, or does every team build it again from scratch?

What I would tell myself in 2023

Drawing these arguments together, the answer to the question appears to be a partial one. Documentary reform and tokenised settlement have begun to reduce paper, reconciliation and delay, and the 2023 article's diagnosis of paper-based processes and costly cross-border payments appears to remain sound. On the evidence reviewed here, however, rebuilt payment infrastructure is unlikely on its own to close the trade-finance gap for smaller firms. The binding constraint appears instead to sit in the cost of establishing and maintaining confidence in a counterparty, together with the question of who remains accountable once another party or system has performed part of that assessment. The revision to the 2023 argument is therefore one of sequence: before proposing a new rail, the first question is why the institution on the existing rail withdrew. The next line of research this raises is how public, authoritative and independently verifiable information might lower the cost of that confidence without creating another opaque score. Legitimate trade may only become easier to finance once the evidence supporting trust can travel as readily as the payment.

The views expressed here are my own and are based on general observations about trade finance, third-party risk and financial inclusion. They draw only on published sources and do not represent the position of the Royal Navy, the Ministry of Defence or any organisation with which I work. Nothing published here reproduces any organisation's internal policy, procedure or assurance output.
