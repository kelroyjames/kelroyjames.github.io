The Rails Were Rebuilt, but the Gap Remained: What Trade Finance Taught Me About Assurance

In 2023 I argued that decentralised finance could help close a gap in trade finance. The rails have since been rebuilt and the gap hasn't moved, which taught me the real problem was never the rails.

Kelroy James Updated September 2026
📖 15 min read 📊 3,450 words
Share on LinkedIn (opens in a new tab) Share on X (opens in a new tab) Share via Email

In 2023 I co-authored an article arguing that decentralised finance could help close a gap in trade finance. The argument came from a practical problem, not an abstract interest in blockchain. The building society that held my mortgage in Saint Vincent had lost its correspondent-banking relationship. Once that relationship disappeared, I could no longer move money into that account from several thousand miles away, even though nothing about my own circumstances had changed. My conclusion at the time was that the architecture itself was the problem. International payments depended on layers of intermediaries, and access to those intermediaries could be withdrawn without appeal.

A decentralised system seemed to offer another route. It promised fewer dependencies, more direct settlement, and less power concentrated at the points a transaction had to pass through. That conclusion was reasonable given what I had just watched happen. But three years is long enough to say plainly that it did not reach the real problem. The intervening years have produced substantial work on digital trade documents, distributed ledgers, tokenised deposits, and faster cross-border settlement, some of it genuinely good. The trade-finance gap has remained close to where it started, and that persistence is the evidence that the rails were only ever part of the problem.

What I had understood as a failure of financial infrastructure turns out to have been an assurance problem as well. A transaction moving through the system was never the only question. The harder question was whether an institution could establish who was actually on the other side of a transaction. It had to do that at a cost it could afford and with confidence it could defend, and then decide whether the risk that came with them was tolerable. That question survives every redesign of the rails, because no rail answers it on its own.

Right about the friction

The original article was right about the friction. Paper documents, fragmented systems, manual reconciliation, and dependence on multiple intermediaries increase cost and delay. Those burdens fall hardest on smaller firms. Their transactions are commercially viable but too expensive for a financial institution to assess and administer at scale. That part of the diagnosis has not changed in three years.

The legal treatment of electronic trade documents has also changed materially since 2023. The UK's Electronic Trade Documents Act 2023 gave a qualifying electronic trade document the same legal effect as its paper equivalent. That closed an old legal gap: an electronic document previously could not be "possessed" the way a paper bill of lading could. It builds on principles the UNCITRAL Model Law on Electronic Transferable Records set out in 2017, giving multiple jurisdictions a common legal model to work from. Digitisation genuinely can reduce delay, duplicate handling, and some forms of documentary risk, and that is a real improvement, not a cosmetic one.

Distributed-ledger and tokenisation initiatives have shown the same willingness to redesign settlement and record-keeping. Banks and financial-market infrastructures are exploring tokenised deposits, shared ledgers, and digitally native forms of commercial-bank money. J.P. Morgan's Kinexys business has already taken deposit tokens into live institutional use through JPM Coin, and Swift's own blockchain-based ledger went live for initial use in July 2026, with seventeen banks across six continents preparing to pilot tokenised cross-border payments on it. The direction of travel is not in doubt: financial infrastructure is becoming more digital, more programmable, and more interconnected. None of that, on its own, demonstrates that better infrastructure produces broader access.

A digital document is easier to transfer than a paper one, but the receiving institution still has to decide whether to rely on it. A tokenised payment can settle faster, but the institution still has to understand the parties, the source of funds, and the exposure attached to them. Technology changes the method of execution. It does nothing, by itself, to resolve the basis on which risk is accepted. That distinction is what the rest of this piece works through.

The problem beneath the payment

The experience behind the 2023 article involved correspondent banking. One financial institution provides services to another, so that smaller or less-connected banks can reach currencies and payment networks they cannot access directly. When a correspondent relationship is withdrawn, the effect rarely stays contained to the respondent bank. It reaches every customer and business that depended on that bank to move money internationally. Published work from the International Monetary Fund, the Financial Stability Board, the Financial Conduct Authority, and the US Department of the Treasury has documented this decline under the label most commonly used for it, de-risking. The FCA's own September 2023 research note makes the point directly: the term covers several different institutional decisions, but the common thread is a bank restricting or ending whole categories of relationship rather than managing each counterparty individually.

It would be too simple to blame regulation alone, and just as misleading to call every withdrawal unjustified. A correspondent bank has to weigh financial-crime exposure, sanctions risk, the quality of the respondent bank's own controls, and the reliability of supervision in its jurisdiction. It also has to weigh the commercial return the relationship actually offers. Where the cost of building sufficient confidence is high and the expected revenue is low, withdrawal becomes a rational institutional decision, even when its wider effects are damaging. That is the calculation that took my mortgage's correspondent bank away in the first place, and no faster settlement rail changes the inputs to it.

That reframed how I read what had happened to me. The payment route had genuinely disappeared. But the correspondent bank no longer had enough confidence in the relationship, or could not maintain that confidence at a cost it was willing to bear. A different settlement mechanism might have moved value without that particular intermediary. It would not have made the underlying institution any easier to assess or any safer to serve. The route was the visible casualty, but the confidence problem underneath it was the actual cause.

The work that did not travel

This distinction matters because financial innovation tends to get evaluated by what it removes. A distributed ledger can remove part of the reconciliation process, and an electronic bill of lading can remove the need to circulate a paper original. Tokenisation works the same way, cutting the delay between conventional messaging and settlement. Each of those is a genuine improvement. None of them removes the need for an institution to make and defend a risk decision about who it is actually dealing with.

Assurance asks a different question: what evidence supports the claim that a person, organisation, asset, or control is what it is represented to be. In correspondent banking and trade finance, that evidence can include beneficial ownership, corporate registration, trading history, control effectiveness, sanctions exposure, and the provenance of the transaction documents themselves. Data existing is not the same as data being usable. An institution also has to know where it came from, whether it is current, what has changed, and how much reliance it can reasonably place on it. That is a harder problem than moving a payment, and it is the one every trade-finance architecture eventually runs into.

This is where my thinking has moved most since 2023. I had treated intermediation mainly as a source of friction, something to be engineered away. In practice, intermediaries also perform assurance work, even when that work is expensive, duplicated, or inconsistently done. They identify parties, examine documents, assess controls, monitor transactions, and retain the evidence behind their decisions. Removing an intermediary relocates that assurance work rather than eliminating it. The new owner of the risk is not always made explicit when the move happens.

"Removing an intermediary relocates that assurance work rather than eliminating it. The new owner of the risk is not always made explicit when the move happens."

The real design question is not whether technology can eliminate trust, but how a system establishes justified confidence, records the basis for it, and assigns responsibility when the evidence turns out to be wrong. An architecture that cannot answer those questions has changed the transaction path without accounting for the assurance work that used to travel along it. The path got faster. The work that used to travel along it did not. That gap is where the trade-finance gap actually lives, and everything that follows is about closing it.

Unchanged since 2023

The trade-finance gap is usually presented as the difference between the demand for trade finance and the amount actually provided. The Asian Development Bank's most recent Global Trade Finance Gap Survey is its ninth. It drew on data from more than 110 trade-finance providers collected between 2023 and 2025, and it put the global gap at $2.5 trillion. That figure is unchanged in absolute terms from 2023, though slightly down as a share of global trade, from 10.6% to roughly 10%. The same survey reported trade-finance rejection rates of 41% for SMEs and 40% for large and mid-cap corporates, the first time those two figures have sat close together rather than SMEs being rejected at a starkly higher rate. The survey publication is here. ADB's January 2026 release is here. A stable, trillion-dollar aggregate over three years is not proof that nothing improved, but it is evidence that whatever improved did not reach the constraint that actually binds.

Not every part of that gap is an assurance failure. Capital constraints, pricing, country risk, currency availability, commercial strategy, and transaction size all affect whether finance gets provided. It would be disproportionate to claim that better verification alone would close a $2.5 trillion gap. The narrower claim is the defensible one: some viable activity stays unfinanced because the cost of establishing confidence in the counterparty is high relative to the value of the relationship. That narrower claim is also the one the SME rejection-rate convergence starts to support, since SMEs are exactly the counterparties a bank knows least about at onboarding.

That cost is not confined to collecting documents in the first place. Institutions regularly receive information that is incomplete, inconsistent across registries, hard to validate across jurisdictions, or simply self-attested. Ownership can also change well after onboarding, while a company stays legally active even as its directors, trading behaviour, or control environment shift underneath it. The assessment is a continuing judgement about whether the evidence still supports the relationship, not a single gateway exercise completed once and forgotten. Large counterparties absorb that ongoing cost more easily, because they have established banking histories, professional compliance functions, and relationships with recognised advisers that smaller firms typically do not.

The result is a structural disadvantage: the parties who most need access are often also the most expensive to understand properly. Faster settlement does nothing to resolve that imbalance, because settlement speed was never the constraint for a counterparty a bank cannot yet confidently assess. The SME rejection rate finally converging with the large-corporate rate is encouraging. But a rate that has converged at roughly 40% either way still leaves most applicants unfinanced. Closing the remaining gap means lowering the cost of understanding a counterparty, not lowering the cost of moving money to one a bank already trusts.

The whole argument in miniature

None of this leads me to dismiss blockchain, tokenisation, or digital trade infrastructure, and I would not want this piece read that way. It leads to a narrower and, I think, more useful expectation of what they can do. Technology helps where it reduces the cost of producing, validating, and maintaining evidence about a counterparty. It does less where it merely accelerates the movement of money once a counterparty is already trusted. That distinction is the whole argument in miniature.

Electronic trade records can improve provenance once their integrity, control, and transfer can actually be demonstrated. Shared ledgers can reduce discrepancies wherever the participants rely on a genuinely common record. Automated checks that flag a change in corporate ownership, sanctions status, or registration data do real work here too. So does public-source intelligence that supplements what a counterparty supplies and directs attention toward the inconsistencies that deserve a closer look. None of these tools removes judgement from the process. Each one, used properly, improves the evidence available to whoever is exercising that judgement.

The practical challenge underneath all of this is reliance. A receiving institution needs to know who collected the information, how identity was established, which sources were actually checked, and when the evidence was last refreshed. If one institution cannot rely on another's verification, digitalisation just reproduces the same checks faster and at greater scale. None of the duplication actually goes away. The process gets more efficient locally while the duplication persists across the whole system, which is progress that looks better on a dashboard than it performs in practice.

Interoperability, for that reason, should not be understood only as a technical property, a question of whether two systems can exchange data. Systems can exchange data successfully while the institutions using them remain unable to accept one another's assessments. That failure mode is more common in practice than an outright technical incompatibility. Meaningful interoperability also needs agreed evidential standards, clear liability, traceable changes, and confidence in the controls that generated the information in the first place. Without those conditions in place, the data moves and the assurance burden stays exactly where it was.

Worth less than it looks

For a financial institution, the first step is separating infrastructure constraints from confidence constraints. A delayed payment, a rejected trade-finance application, or a terminated correspondent relationship can look, on the surface, like a problem of network access. The institution should test that assumption directly. Was the decisive constraint really about reaching the network? Or was it uncertainty about the counterparty, the reliability of its controls, or the strength of the evidence available to support continued risk acceptance? Most institutions have never actually asked that question of their own rejected applications.

The next step is identifying where assurance costs are actually being created. Repeated requests for the same information, manual comparison of inconsistent records, and reliance on documents that go stale within months of onboarding all raise the cost of maintaining a relationship. Nobody usually tracks that cost as a single line item. Mapping those points shows where a digital tool would genuinely improve the underlying decision, rather than simply automating an inefficiency that was never worth keeping. That mapping exercise, done honestly, usually surprises the people who commission it.

Institutions should also learn to distinguish evidence from assertion. A completed questionnaire records what a counterparty says about itself. It does not establish that the statement is accurate or still current. Independent registries, regulatory records, litigation data, sanctions sources, and adverse-media checks can each provide corroboration, though every source has limitations and has to be used proportionately to the risk in front of it. The objective is not to accumulate more data for its own sake but to improve the basis on which a material claim gets accepted.

Finally, ownership of the decision has to stay clear no matter how much of the process gets automated. Technology can collect evidence, flag inconsistencies, or calculate a risk indicator. But the institution still needs to know who is authorised to accept the relationship, what conditions attach to that acceptance, and when the decision has to be revisited. An automated process running without explicit authority and accountability behind it can make a weak assessment look more consistent without making it any more defensible. Consistency and defensibility are not the same property, and a dashboard that only delivers the first one is worth less than it looks.

What I would tell myself in 2023

The original argument began with a real failure of access. A small institution had lost a necessary banking channel, and a financial architecture with fewer intermediaries looked capable of solving that problem. I would not now reject that reasoning entirely. Dependence on a small number of correspondent institutions genuinely does create vulnerability, and alternative settlement forms can improve resilience in some circumstances. What I would change is the order in which I asked the questions.

I would ask a different question before proposing a new rail: what actually caused the institution on the existing rail to withdraw. If the answer concerned settlement cost or technical reach, a different piece of infrastructure might genuinely fix it. If the answer concerned ownership transparency, financial-crime controls, supervisory confidence, or the cost of maintaining reliable information, the rail was never the principal problem, and the institution's ability to establish and sustain justified confidence in the party using it was. Asking which of those two answers actually applies takes an afternoon of honest diagnosis, not a new settlement architecture.

That distinction changes where the intervention should go. Instead of assuming that removing intermediaries automatically produces inclusion, attention shifts to the quality, provenance, and portability of the evidence those intermediaries were producing. It asks whether a reliable assessment can be reused rather than repeated. It also asks whether a change in a counterparty's circumstances can be detected sooner, and whether a smaller counterparty can demonstrate control effectiveness without reproducing the compliance infrastructure of a global bank just to be taken seriously. None of those questions needed a new financial architecture, only an industry willing to stop treating assurance as overhead.

"What my mortgage's correspondent bank actually needed was not a transaction that moved faster or looked more novel, but another institution able to understand the risk, obtain sufficient evidence, and stay willing to own the decision."

This is not as dramatic as rebuilding global finance, and it is a great deal closer to the problem I actually encountered. What my mortgage's correspondent bank actually needed was not a transaction that moved faster or looked more novel, but another institution able to understand the risk, obtain sufficient evidence, and stay willing to own the decision once it had that evidence. It is a narrower ask than a new architecture. It was the one thing actually missing in 2023, and it still is.

Where the argument now leads

The evidence since 2023 supports a restrained conclusion rather than a dramatic one. Digital trade legislation, tokenisation initiatives, and new settlement models have shown that large parts of financial infrastructure can genuinely be redesigned. They have reduced paper handling, reconciliation, and delay along the way. What none of it has demonstrated is that infrastructure reform alone resolves the exclusion smaller firms, banks, and jurisdictions actually experience. The $2.5 trillion gap sitting almost exactly where it sat three years ago is the evidence for that claim, not an assumption behind it.

What remains unresolved is the cost of knowing enough about a counterparty to make a defensible decision about them. That cost includes obtaining the evidence, testing its reliability, and monitoring it for change. It also includes working out who stays accountable once another party or system has done part of the assessment. Unless that cost falls, without weakening the standard of judgement behind it, improved rails will keep carrying transactions mainly for the counterparties a bank could already assess with confidence. Everyone else stays exactly where the 2023 article found them.

My next practical question is narrower than the one the original article posed. How can institutions use public, authoritative, and independently verifiable information to strengthen counterparty assessment, without creating another opaque score or another layer of duplicated due diligence on top of the old one? That question connects trade-finance access to the wider disciplines of third-party risk and assurance I have written about elsewhere on this site, and it is a more useful question than the one I started with. It also preserves the part of the original argument that still holds: financial systems should make legitimate activity easier to finance. They will not do that sustainably until the evidence supporting trust can travel as effectively as the payment does.

Questions for the institution

  • Which of our financing or correspondent decisions are actually limited by settlement infrastructure, and which by the cost of knowing the counterparty?
  • What evidence about a counterparty could we obtain more cheaply than we do now, and who would need to accept it?
  • Where do we rely on self-attestation, and what would independent, public-source verification add?
  • Can a reliable assessment we have already made be reused elsewhere in the institution, or does every team have to build it again from scratch?

The views expressed here are my own and are based on general observations about trade finance, third-party risk and financial inclusion. They draw only on published sources and do not represent the position of the Royal Navy, the Ministry of Defence or any organisation with which I work. Nothing published here reproduces any organisation's internal policy, procedure or assurance output.
