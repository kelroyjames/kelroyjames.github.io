The Rails Were Rebuilt, but the Gap Remained: What Trade Finance Taught Me About Assurance

In 2023 I argued that decentralised finance could help close a gap in trade finance. The rails have since been rebuilt and the gap hasn't moved, which taught me the real problem was never the rails.

Kelroy James Updated September 2026
📖 10 min read 📊 1,847 words
Share on LinkedIn (opens in a new tab) Share on X (opens in a new tab) Share via Email

What does a faster payment rail change for someone whose bank has lost the relationship that connected it to the rest of the world? For me the question arrived as a practical problem before it became an intellectual one. The building society that held my mortgage in Saint Vincent lost its correspondent-banking relationship, and with it went my ability to move money into that account from several thousand miles away, although nothing about my own circumstances had changed. An article I co-authored for Trade Finance Global in January 2023 drew the conclusion that seemed obvious at the time: international payments depended on layers of intermediaries whose support could be withdrawn without appeal, and a decentralised architecture offered a route with fewer of them.

Three years is long enough to test that conclusion against what followed. The period since has produced serious work on digital trade documents, distributed ledgers, tokenised deposits and faster cross-border settlement, and the trade-finance gap has nonetheless stayed close to where it started. Read alongside eighteen years of running accounts that only balance when someone can vouch for what is in them, that persistence points somewhere the 2023 article did not look. The binding constraint appears to be assurance: whether an institution can establish who sits on the other side of a transaction, at a cost it can bear and with a confidence it can defend. A new rail carries that question forward without answering it.

Right about the friction

The original diagnosis of friction holds up. Paper documents, fragmented systems, manual reconciliation and dependence on several intermediaries all add cost and delay, and those costs fall hardest on smaller firms whose transactions are viable but expensive to assess. The World Trade Organization was already describing gaps in trade-finance provision for SMEs in 2016, so the problem long predates the recent wave of technology. The legal position has since moved in a helpful direction. The UK's Electronic Trade Documents Act 2023 gave a qualifying electronic trade document the same legal effect as its paper equivalent, resolving the old difficulty that an electronic record could not be "possessed" in the way a paper bill of lading could, and it built on UNCITRAL's 2017 Model Law on Electronic Transferable Records.

Settlement infrastructure has moved just as visibly. J.P. Morgan's Kinexys business has taken deposit tokens into live institutional use through JPM Coin, and Swift's blockchain-based ledger went live for initial use in July 2026, with seventeen banks across six continents preparing to pilot tokenised cross-border payments on it. These are substantial achievements in execution, and they leave in place the decision that precedes execution. A digital document is only as useful as a receiving institution's willingness to rely on it, and a tokenised payment still requires that institution to understand the parties, the source of funds and the exposure it is accepting.

The problem beneath the payment

Correspondent banking allows smaller or less-connected banks to reach currencies and payment networks through a larger institution, and when the relationship is withdrawn the consequences travel outward to every business and household that relied on it. The literature on this decline is extensive. The International Monetary Fund made the case for policy action on withdrawn correspondent relationships in 2016, and the US Treasury published a de-risking strategy in April 2023. The Financial Conduct Authority's research note of the same year identifies the common thread as banks restricting whole categories of relationship instead of assessing each counterparty on its merits. The Caribbean recurs as a case throughout, to the point that CSIS has asked whether de-risking there has become a "new normal".

De Koker and Casanovas frame the phenomenon as a possible over-compliance dilemma, and the framing is useful because it resists two easy explanations: regulation alone does not account for it, and nor does every withdrawal reflect poor judgement. A correspondent bank must weigh financial-crime exposure, sanctions risk, the quality of the respondent's controls, the reliability of supervision in its jurisdiction and the commercial return on offer. The cost of reaching a defensible view on those questions is largely fixed per relationship, while the revenue scales with the relationship's size. Where the first outweighs the second, withdrawal is a rational decision, however damaging its wider effects. On my reading, that calculation is what took my mortgage's correspondent route away, and a different settlement mechanism would have left the respondent bank no easier to assess.

The work that did not travel

Anyone who has taken over a stores account knows the difference between a ledger and a verified ledger. In naval logistics an incoming custodian accepts an account only after it has been audited, because signing for someone else's record is an act of reliance, and reliance needs evidence behind it. Correspondent banking rests on the same logic at a larger scale. A bank processing payments for another relies on that institution's due diligence, controls and supervision, and it carries the consequences if the reliance proves misplaced.

Financial innovation tends to be judged by what it removes: a reconciliation step, a paper original, a delay between messaging and settlement. Assurance asks what evidence supports the claim that a person, organisation, asset or control is what it is represented to be. In trade finance that evidence spans beneficial ownership, registration, trading history, control effectiveness, sanctions exposure and document provenance, and it must be current and traceable before anyone can reasonably act on it. The 2023 article treated intermediation mainly as friction. The stronger view, three years on, is that intermediaries also perform assurance work, however expensively or inconsistently, by identifying parties, examining documents, monitoring transactions and retaining the evidence behind each decision.

"Removing an intermediary relocates the assurance work it was doing. The new owner of that risk is rarely named when the move happens."

Unchanged since 2023

The Asian Development Bank's ninth Global Trade Finance Gap Survey, drawing on more than 110 trade-finance providers between 2023 and 2025, put the global gap at $2.5 trillion. The figure is unchanged in absolute terms since 2023 and only slightly lower as a share of global trade, down from 10.6% to roughly 10%. The bank's January 2026 release reported rejection rates of 41% for SMEs and 40% for large and mid-cap corporates, the first time the two figures have sat so close together. A stable aggregate does not prove that nothing improved, though it does suggest that whatever improved has not yet reached the constraint that binds.

The claim needs to stay proportionate. Capital, pricing, country risk, currency availability and transaction size all shape whether finance is provided, and better verification alone could not plausibly close a gap of $2.5 trillion. The narrower claim, and the one the evidence supports, is that some viable activity goes unfinanced because establishing confidence in the counterparty costs more than the relationship is worth. That cost continues after onboarding, since ownership, directors and trading behaviour can change while a company remains legally active. Large counterparties absorb it through established banking histories and professional compliance functions, which leaves the firms that most need access among the most expensive to understand.

The whole argument in miniature

None of this amounts to a case against blockchain, tokenisation or digital trade infrastructure. It is a case for a narrower expectation of them. Technology earns its place where it lowers the cost of producing, validating and maintaining evidence about a counterparty, and contributes least where it only accelerates payments between parties who already trust one another. Electronic trade records can strengthen provenance, shared ledgers reduce discrepancies where participants rely on a common record, and automated checks and public-source verification can test what a counterparty says about itself.

Supply chain assurance offers a close parallel. A supplier questionnaire records what a supplier says about itself, a goods-in check tests whether the delivery matches the promise, and a risk programme built only on the first has a gap it cannot see. Trade finance faces the same distinction between assertion and evidence, compounded by reliance: if one institution cannot rely on another's verification, digitalisation simply reproduces the same checks faster. Genuine interoperability therefore depends on agreed evidential standards, clear liability and confidence in the controls that generated the information, well beyond two systems exchanging data.

Worth less than it looks

For a financial institution, the practical starting point is separating infrastructure constraints from confidence constraints. A declined application or a terminated correspondent relationship can present as a problem of network access, and the institution's own files will usually show whether the decisive factor was reaching the network or uncertainty about the counterparty. Mapping where assurance cost accumulates, in repeated requests for the same information and documents that go stale within months, shows where a digital tool would improve a decision instead of automating an inefficiency. Ownership of the decision must also stay explicit however much of the process is automated. An automated assessment without clear authority behind it can look more consistent while becoming no more defensible, and a dashboard that delivers consistency alone is worth less than it looks.

What I would tell myself in 2023

The 2023 reasoning does not deserve to be discarded. Dependence on a small number of correspondent institutions genuinely creates vulnerability, and alternative settlement can improve resilience. What deserves revision is the order of the questions. Before proposing a new rail, the first question is what caused the institution on the existing rail to withdraw. If the answer concerns settlement cost or technical reach, new infrastructure may help. If it concerns ownership transparency, financial-crime controls or the cost of reliable information, the intervention belongs in the quality and portability of evidence, and in whether a smaller counterparty can demonstrate its controls without replicating a global bank's compliance function.

The evidence since 2023 supports a restrained conclusion. Digital trade law, tokenisation and new settlement models have shown that much of the financial infrastructure can be redesigned. Whether redesign alone can resolve the exclusion experienced by smaller firms, banks and jurisdictions remains unproven. The unresolved problem is the cost of knowing enough about a counterparty to make a defensible decision, including who remains accountable once another party or system has performed part of the assessment. The question worth carrying forward is how institutions might use public, authoritative and independently verifiable information to strengthen that assessment without creating another opaque score. Legitimate trade will be easier to finance once the evidence supporting trust can travel as readily as the payment does.

An afternoon of honest diagnosis

Most of that diagnosis can begin in an institution's own files, and it needs an afternoon of honest review well before it needs a new settlement architecture.

  • Which of our financing or correspondent decisions are limited by settlement infrastructure, and which by the cost of knowing the counterparty?
  • What evidence about a counterparty could we obtain more cheaply than we do now, and who would need to accept it?
  • Where do we rely on self-attestation, and what would independent, public-source verification add?
  • Can an assessment we have already made be reused elsewhere in the institution, or does every team build it again from scratch?

The views expressed here are my own and are based on general observations about trade finance, third-party risk and financial inclusion. They draw only on published sources and do not represent the position of the Royal Navy, the Ministry of Defence or any organisation with which I work. Nothing published here reproduces any organisation's internal policy, procedure or assurance output.
