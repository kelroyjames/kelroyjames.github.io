The three lines of defence: from warships to AI

Applying military-grade logistics assurance (Tier 1–3) to AI model governance (ISO/IEC 42001). The Royal Navy's four-tier assurance model and the ISO standard governing AI management systems turn out to be solving the same problem, in different vocabularies.

I found this mapping by accident. I was partway through an ISO/IEC 27001 Lead Auditor course when I recognised a control I had been running for years under a completely different name. The Royal Navy's four-tier assurance model, made up of sub-departmental checks, departmental review, independent inspection, and strategic governance, is not a naval invention. It is the same architecture ISO management-system standards use everywhere, described in a vocabulary built for warships instead of boardrooms.

That recognition is what "Same Control, Two Vocabularies" is about at the level of individual tasks. This presentation, given to the Global Skill Development Council, takes the same argument up a level: to the assurance architecture itself, applied to a live governance problem: AI model risk under ISO/IEC 42001.

If a four-tier model has kept warships and their supply chains audit-ready for decades, the same structure is a reasonable starting point for governing something that changes as fast as an AI model does.

The foundation

Royal Navy BRd 93

BRd 93 structures naval logistics assurance into four tiers: routine sub-departmental checks, departmental review by a Logistics Officer applying professional judgement, independent inspection by Fleet Logistics Inspection teams, and strategic oversight at Navy Command level. Each tier exists to catch what the tier below it cannot see from where it stands.

The mechanism that makes this work at handover is Supersession: a mandatory deep-dive audit before a new officer takes charge of an account, so risk is baselined explicitly rather than inherited blind. No one takes command of an unknown risk position.

The comparison

BRd 93 vs. ISO/IEC 42001

Line them up and the architecture matches almost exactly. Where BRd 93 has a Logistics Officer applying professional judgement to risk context, ISO/IEC 42001 has an AI Risk Officer providing human oversight under Clause 5. Where the Navy runs Supersession at every handover, the standard runs mandatory model supersession whenever a model owner changes, a model moves from staging to production, or its training data changes materially.

Military assurance is stronger on enforcing accountability through binary grading and mandatory handover. ISO 42001 is stronger on managing a lifecycle that keeps changing after deployment. Neither replaces the other: the strongest AI governance model applies Navy-grade accountability inside ISO's more flexible structure.

The map

Naval tiers → corporate defence

Translated into the language a corporate risk function already uses: Tier 1 (the unit) and Tier 2 (the Logistics Officer) map to the First Line: the developers and risk owners doing the work and making the first judgement call. Tier 3 (Fleet Logistics Inspection) maps to the Second Line: compliance and risk oversight, independent of the people being checked. Tier 4 (Navy Command) maps to the Third Line: internal audit and the governance board setting risk appetite from a position of genuine independence.

The tiers in practice

Operational control → independent assurance

Tier 1: Operational Control. On a unit, this is sub-departmental daily checks, often delegated to junior ratings as part of their training. In AI development, the equivalent is automated CI/CD pipelines, unit tests, and the discipline of treating compliance as "code-first": developers own the initial risk assessment, and the definition of done includes governance, not just function.

Tier 2: Professional Judgement. A Logistics Officer doesn't just re-check Tier 1's box-ticking; they apply judgement to decide whether the underlying risk is acceptable. The AI Risk Officer does the same thing looking at bias metrics and drift analysis: not ticking boxes, but deciding whether what the numbers show is a risk worth escalating.

Tier 3: Independent Assurance. Fleet Logistics Inspection uses a binary grading system running from Fully Compliant to No Compliance: there is no "partially safe" grade, because in a warzone or a regulated market, partial safety isn't a real category. ISO 42001's internal audit under Clause 9.2 exists for the same reason: independent verification of the safety guardrails, not a self-assessment.

Two lessons that transfer directly

From the deck

Lesson 1: The Supersession check. The Navy protocol is simple: no inheritance of unknown risks. A new officer does not accept an account without a deep-dive audit first. Applied to AI, that means mandatory supersession whenever the model owner changes, the model moves from staging to production, or the training data or service history needs revalidating. The principle is the same: don't inherit a risk position you haven't verified yourself.

Lesson 2: Minimum Viable Bureaucracy. Not every check deserves the same weight. The Navy re-categorised its checks into Governance (lawful obligations, non-negotiable), Assurance (enablers for operational capability) and Compliance (internal checks, now trusted to the inspector's risk-based discretion), and started accepting "Checked, NSTR" (Nothing Significant to Report) as a valid log entry where the risk is genuinely low. One naval streamlining exercise reported a 50% reduction in documentation burden this way. The same discipline applies to AI governance: don't document every low-risk parameter change. Focus the energy on the high-risk governance items, such as legal, safety, ethics and bias thresholds, and automate the rest.

Why it matters

Assurance as capability

Logistics assurance isn't paperwork bolted onto operations: it's what gives command the confidence to deploy in the first place. As AI moves into supply chains through predictive maintenance and autonomous resupply, it introduces probabilistic risk into what used to be a deterministic system. The stakes are the same shape either way: we need the same level of trust in our algorithms that we already require of our physical stores. If AI is the engine, governance is the steering wheel.

The summary is deliberately simple. First Line: operational judgement, with the shop floor owning the risk. Second Line: inspections with teeth, graded assurance that can force immediate action when something is genuinely unsatisfactory. Third Line: strategic direction, where a governance board sets risk appetite and owns the supersession policy. It is the structure the Royal Navy has used to keep complex, high-consequence systems accountable for decades, applied to a governance problem that is, at bottom, the same problem in new clothes.
