The Physical Assurance Gap in Third-Party Risk

Two 2026 KPMG surveys map where third-party risk management is putting its attention: data, AI, managed services, integration with enterprise risk. Physical verification barely appears in either, and the people already doing it have never been told that it counts.

Kelroy James Updated September 2026
📖 9 min read 📊 1,754 words
Share on LinkedIn (opens in a new tab) Share on X (opens in a new tab) Share via Email

A goods-in inspector at a distribution site checks a delivery against the purchase order: quantity, condition, country-of-origin marking, batch numbers where they matter. The inspector flags a mismatch, logs it and moves on to the next pallet. Nobody calls this governance, risk or compliance work, least of all the person doing it. I ran a version of that same check on a naval stores account for years, and setting the practice against the third-party risk literature showed me what it is in the language of every framework in use today: first-line control testing, independent verification that what a supplier promised on paper is what actually arrived.

KPMG published two global surveys in 2026 that together give the clearest picture yet of where third-party risk management is spending its attention. They describe a discipline investing in data, artificial intelligence, integration with enterprise risk and outsourced monitoring. Physical verification, the kind the goods-in inspector performs several times an hour, barely appears in either as a named category of assurance activity. That absence is worth examining, because the case for treating it as central sits inside the same two surveys.

Where the attention and the money are going

KPMG's global TPRM survey drew on 851 professionals across industries and regions. Regulatory compliance and cyber risk dominate strategy, cited by 45 and 48 percent of respondents. Risk assessment and due diligence, and TPRM technology, top the spending list at 52 and 51 percent, and more than 80 percent of organisations already outsource core TPRM activities. The companion survey of 725 Chief Ethics and Compliance Officers tells the same story from the compliance side: 77 percent expect more investment in data analytics, 75 percent in cybersecurity and data privacy, and 64 percent of US respondents name third-party risk management as a target for efficiency gains.

Both surveys also record where that investment is not yet delivering. Only 17 percent of TPRM respondents report the highest tier of data quality, and just 22 percent find their use of AI "very effective" while 50 to 58 percent say they use it. Only 22 percent of compliance leaders are "highly confident" in their organisation's ability to identify and address vendor risk holistically. The TPRM report is candid about one cause: most organisations try to screen every third party, although KPMG's experience puts the genuinely high-risk share at 10 to 20 percent.

I do not read either survey as mistaken. Data quality, AI and risk-based screening are real problems with real evidence behind them. What I miss, from the other side of a goods-in desk, is any sustained treatment of physical verification as its own category of evidence, distinct from the questionnaires, certifications and monitoring dashboards both surveys treat as the whole picture. The surveys describe where the industry's attention has settled, and attention that settles long enough starts to look like the whole picture.

The gap the data keeps pointing at

The surveys keep gesturing towards this gap without naming it. The TPRM report calls Nth-party visibility, understanding the suppliers an organisation's own suppliers depend on, a critical blind spot, and gives concentration in a single geography as its standard example. Concentration in a geography is a physical fact about where a factory, a warehouse or a single access road sits. Confirming it requires someone to have looked, or to have arranged for someone credible to look.

The same report notes that procurement systems in different countries often fail to roll up into one global view, and frames that as an integration failure. Read another way, it describes risk data originating on the ground, site by site, before it reaches any system built to aggregate it. If the frontline capture is weak, no downstream integration recovers what was never recorded accurately. Fixing the pipe does nothing for water that was never collected at the source.

The outsourcing figures point the same way. Forty-four percent of organisations use managed services for ongoing monitoring and 27 percent outsource due diligence, both concentrated at the paperwork-heavy stages of the lifecycle. End-to-end managed services, the model closest to continuous independent oversight of a supplier including its physical operations, sit at 5 percent. The industry has built substantial capacity to process what a supplier says about itself, and comparatively little to verify it in person.

Without a unified view of your third parties, you can't possibly have a unified view of your risk.

That line comes from the TPRM survey itself, offered about fragmented procurement systems. It applies equally to a fragmented view of evidence types. A risk register built entirely from self-reported data and system-generated monitoring has a unified view of one kind of signal and no view of another.

The site that stopped shipping

Three trends in the same surveys suggest physical verification will matter more in the next few years. The first is disruption. A third of organisations suffered monetary loss or reputational damage from a third-party failure in the past three years, and 28 percent faced a supply chain disruption. Disruptions like these are rarely first detected in a data field. They show up as a site that has stopped shipping, a warehouse that has flooded or a factory that has changed ownership without anyone updating the vendor record.

The second is regulation. The TPRM survey's regulatory map lists telecommunications supply chain security rules, critical infrastructure protection and operational resilience regimes across the US, UK, EU and Asia-Pacific, several of which expect organisations to understand physical supply chain structure as well as contractual and cyber posture. A framework built to satisfy them from documentation alone will struggle the first time a regulator asks how a finding was verified.

The third is AI, and it is the one the surveys least expect. Generative tools make a polished, plausible self-attestation cheaper to produce than ever. A questionnaire response, a certificate or a monitoring report can be assembled to look complete without the underlying practice being sound. As the cost of convincing paperwork falls, its evidentiary value falls with it, and the value of a fact nobody can fabricate from a desk rises: that the delivery matched the manifest, and that the site looked the way the questionnaire described it. The compliance survey records the tension without resolving it, with 68 percent of compliance leaders "mixed, leaning positive" about AI and most organisations yet to meet the data-quality prerequisite for using it well.

The people already doing it

The function already exists. Setting my own stores-account practice against the TPRM literature is what showed me how much front-line physical work already does third-party risk control, carried out by people whose job titles never mention it.

Physical tasks that are already third-party risk controls

Front-line physical tasks that are already third-party risk activities, and what each one actually is.
Front-line taskWhat it actually is
Checking a delivery against the purchase order and packing listFirst-line control testing of a supplier's stated output against physical reality
A quality inspector's site visit to a supplier's premisesIndependent third-party monitoring, the physical equivalent of a desk-based audit
A driver or handler noting a supplier's yard conditions, stock levels, or staffing on a routine collectionAn early-warning input to the risk register, generated incidentally on a routine collection
A warehouse supervisor querying a country-of-origin marking that does not match the paperworkA due-diligence exception, of exactly the kind a compliance function exists to investigate
Confirming batch or serial numbers against a shipping manifest before sign-offVerification of a supplier's chain of custody, not merely a stores formality

Every row in that table happens today in most organisations without going near a risk register. The observation is made, the exception is raised through an operational channel and resolved as an operational matter, and the risk function never learns it existed. A supplier that has failed the same physical check three times this year looks, from the register, exactly like a supplier that has never been checked, because the evidence lives in a different system owned by a different function.

A field, a route, a list

Closing the gap means extending KPMG's own TPRM recommendations to a category of evidence the surveys did not have in view, using channels that already exist. No new department is needed, and nobody has to generate more evidence. Each action routes evidence that is already being produced:

  • Name physical verification as its own evidence type in the risk register schema, alongside questionnaire responses and system-generated monitoring
  • Route front-line exceptions to the same escalation path as a flagged due-diligence finding, so a repeated physical discrepancy accumulates in the record
  • Give front-line staff a short, specific list of what counts as reportable, so the person who already notices something knows it matters
  • Weight independent, in-person verification towards the Critical tier, applying KPMG's own "focus your firepower" logic to physical checks
  • Treat a physical exception as evidence that corroborates or contradicts the supplier's self-attestation, the same discipline good open-source verification applies to a filing or an ownership record

The cost is small relative to the managed-services spend both surveys describe. The real decision is whether evidence generated on a loading dock is admissible in the same conversation as evidence generated by a platform. I have seen what that changes. On a Royal Navy supply chain account, physically locating equipment recorded as lost let me close most of fourteen major loss investigations by recovery, and a recovered item closes without leaving a value gap in the account. The case study sets out how it was done.

Failed three times, or never checked

  • Where does physical evidence enter our risk register today, and in which field?
  • When a goods-in or site-visit exception is raised, who outside operations ever hears about it?
  • Can the register tell a supplier that has never been physically checked from one that has failed a check three times this year?
  • Which Critical-tier suppliers have had an independent, in-person check in the last twelve months?

The check that goes unseen

The goods-in inspector will check the next delivery whether or not anyone names what the inspector is doing. That check happens thousands of times a day in every organisation with a physical supply chain, and it usually ends where it started, resolved as an operational matter and unseen by the risk function. The people closing the physical assurance gap are already in post, and a programme's task is to make sure what they see reaches the register. KPMG's two surveys made that case without meaning to, by describing everything the discipline measures except the evidence sitting closest to the ground.
