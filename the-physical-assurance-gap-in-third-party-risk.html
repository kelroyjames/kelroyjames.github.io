The Physical Assurance Gap in Third-Party Risk

Two 2026 KPMG surveys map where third-party risk management is putting its attention: data, AI, managed services, integration with enterprise risk. Physical verification barely appears in either, and the people already doing it have never been told that it counts.

Kelroy James Updated September 2026
📖 10 min read 📊 ~2,360 words
Share on LinkedIn (opens in a new tab) Share on X (opens in a new tab) Share via Email

A goods-in inspector at a distribution site checks a delivery against the purchase order: quantity, condition, country-of-origin marking, batch numbers where they matter. The inspector flags a mismatch, logs it, and moves on to the next pallet. Nobody calls this governance, risk or compliance work, least of all the person doing it. I spent years running a version of that same check on a naval stores account, long before anyone gave it a name more formal than doing the job properly. It is also, in the language of every third-party risk framework in use today, first-line control testing: independent verification that what a supplier promised on paper is what actually arrived.

KPMG published two global surveys in 2026 that, between them, offer the clearest picture yet of where third-party risk management is spending its attention. Read together, they describe a discipline pouring resource into data, artificial intelligence, integration with enterprise risk, and outsourced monitoring. Physical verification, the kind the goods-in inspector performs several times an hour, is almost absent from either report as a named category of assurance activity. That absence is worth examining, because the case for treating it as central is sitting inside the same two surveys that overlook it.

Where the attention and the money are going

KPMG's global TPRM survey gathered responses from 851 professionals across industries and regions. Its headline findings are about scale and technology: regulatory compliance and cyber risk dominate strategy, cited by 45 and 48 percent of respondents respectively; risk assessment and due diligence, and TPRM technology, top the spending list at 52 and 51 percent; more than 80 percent of organisations already use managed services or outsourcing for core TPRM activities. The companion KPMG survey of 725 Chief Ethics and Compliance Officers tells a consistent story from the compliance side: 77 percent anticipate additional investment in data analytics, 75 percent in cybersecurity and data privacy, and third-party risk management ranks among the top areas targeted for process efficiency gains over the next two years, cited by 64 percent of US respondents. Both surveys describe the same discipline reaching for the same tools, from two different vantage points.

Both surveys also record where that investment is not yet delivering. Only 17 percent of TPRM respondents report the highest tier of data quality, and just 22 percent find their use of AI "very effective" despite 50 to 58 percent claiming to use it. Only 22 percent of CCOs describe themselves as "highly confident" in their organisation's ability to identify and address vendor risk holistically, rather than by individual business unit. The TPRM survey's own recommendation roundup is candid about the underlying cause: most organisations attempt to screen every third party on their books, even though KPMG's own experience puts the genuinely high-risk share at 10 to 20 percent. Effort is going everywhere at once, and the survey's advice is to concentrate it.

None of that is wrong, and I do not read either survey as mistaken. Data quality, AI orchestration, and risk-based screening are all real problems with real evidence behind them. What the diagnosis lacks, and what I recognise from the other side of a goods-in desk, is any sustained treatment of physical verification as its own category of assurance evidence, distinct from the questionnaire responses, certifications, and monitoring dashboards both surveys treat as the whole of the picture. That gap is not a criticism of the surveys' methodology so much as a description of where the industry's attention has settled, and attention that settles somewhere for long enough starts to look like the whole picture even when it is not.

The gap the data itself keeps pointing at

Look past the headline percentages and the surveys keep gesturing towards exactly this gap without naming it. The TPRM report identifies "Nth-party" visibility, understanding the suppliers an organisation's own suppliers depend on, as a critical blind spot, and gives concentration in a single geography as the standard example. Concentration in a geography is a physical fact about where a factory, a warehouse, or a single access road sits, and no dashboard resolves it on its own. Confirming it requires someone to have looked, or to have arranged for someone credible to look on your behalf.

The same report notes that procurement systems in different countries often fail to roll up to a single global view, which it frames as an integration failure. It is also, read differently, a description of the fact that risk data originates on the ground, country by country, site by site, generated by people running local processes, before it ever reaches a system built to aggregate it. If the frontline capture is weak, no amount of downstream integration recovers what was never recorded accurately in the first place. Fixing the pipe does nothing for water that was never collected at the source.

The outsourcing data tells a related story. Forty-four percent of organisations use managed services for ongoing monitoring and 27 percent outsource due diligence, both concentrated at the paperwork-heavy stages of the lifecycle. End-to-end managed services, the model closest to genuinely independent, continuous oversight of a supplier relationship including its physical operations, sit at just 5 percent adoption. The industry has built substantial capacity to process what a supplier says about itself. It has built comparatively little to verify it in person.

Without a unified view of your third parties, you can't possibly have a unified view of your risk.

That line comes from the TPRM survey itself, offered in the context of fragmented procurement systems. It applies with equal force to a fragmented view of evidence types. A risk register built entirely from self-reported data and system-generated monitoring has a unified view of one kind of signal and no view at all of another. The survey's own language describes the physical blind spot more precisely than the survey itself seems to notice.

The site that stopped shipping

Three trends inside the same two surveys point towards physical verification mattering more in the next few years, not less. The first is disruption itself. A third of organisations surveyed suffered monetary loss or reputational damage from a third-party failure in the past three years, and 28 percent faced a supply chain disruption. Disruptions of that kind are rarely first detected in a data field. They show up as a site that has stopped shipping, a warehouse that has flooded, a factory that has changed ownership without anyone updating the vendor record.

The second is regulatory. The TPRM survey's own regulatory map lists telecommunications supply chain security rules, critical infrastructure protection requirements, and operational resilience regimes spanning the US, UK, EU, and Asia-Pacific, several of which explicitly require organisations to understand physical supply chain structure, not only contractual or cyber posture. A compliance framework built to satisfy those requirements from documentation alone will find the documentation insufficient the first time a regulator asks how a finding was actually verified. Documentation describes what the organisation believes, while verification is what a regulator actually wants demonstrated.

The third is AI itself, and this is the one the surveys least expect. Generative tools make a polished, plausible self-attestation cheaper to produce than at any point before. A questionnaire response, a certificate, even a monitoring report can now be assembled to look complete without the underlying practice being sound. I have watched this happen: a well-formatted report has never been proof that anyone actually looked. As the cost of producing convincing paperwork falls, the evidentiary value of paperwork falls with it, and the evidentiary value of a fact nobody can fabricate from a desk, that the delivery actually matched the manifest, that the site actually looked the way the questionnaire described it, rises correspondingly. The CCO survey records this tension without resolving it: 68 percent of compliance leaders feel "mixed, leaning positive" about AI, and the same report flags data quality as a prerequisite for effective AI use that most organisations have not yet met. Leaning further into digital assurance without also strengthening the physical check behind it addresses the wrong half of that trust problem.

The people already closing it, without the title

The function already exists, staffed by people who have never been given the vocabulary for what they do. I was one of those people once, and it took years before anyone called what I was doing assurance.

Physical tasks that are already third-party risk controls

Front-line physical tasks that are already third-party risk activities, and what each one actually is.
Front-line taskWhat it actually is
Checking a delivery against the purchase order and packing listFirst-line control testing of a supplier's stated output against physical reality
A quality inspector's site visit to a supplier's premisesIndependent third-party monitoring, the physical equivalent of a desk-based audit
A driver or handler noting a supplier's yard conditions, stock levels, or staffing on a routine collectionAn early-warning input to the risk register, generated incidentally on a routine collection
A warehouse supervisor querying a country-of-origin marking that does not match the paperworkA due-diligence exception, of exactly the kind a compliance function exists to investigate
Confirming batch or serial numbers against a shipping manifest before sign-offVerification of a supplier's chain of custody, not merely a stores formality

Every row in that table happens today, in most organisations, without being routed anywhere near a risk register. The observation is made, the exception is raised through an operational channel, it gets resolved as an operational matter, and the risk function never learns it existed. A supplier who has failed the same physical check three times this year looks, from the risk register's perspective, exactly like a supplier who has never been checked at all, because the evidence lives in a different system, owned by a different function, and was never designed to travel. Those two suppliers should never look the same to a programme that claims to know its risk.

A field, a route, a list

Closing it means extending KPMG's own TPRM recommendations to a category of evidence the surveys did not have in view, using channels that already exist. No new physical assurance department needs building, and no compliance manual needs handing to goods-in staff. Every action below routes evidence that is already being generated, rather than asking anyone to generate more of it. Each one is a small change to where existing information travels, not a new activity for anyone to perform.

  • Name physical verification as its own evidence type in the risk register schema, alongside questionnaire responses and system-generated monitoring data
  • Route front-line exceptions to the same escalation path as a flagged due-diligence finding, so a repeated physical discrepancy accumulates in the record
  • Give front-line staff a short, specific list of what counts as reportable, so the person who already notices something can recognise that it matters without needing to be told twice
  • Weight independent, in-person verification towards the Critical tier specifically, applying KPMG's own "focus your firepower" logic to physical checks
  • Treat a physical exception as corroborating or contradicting evidence against the supplier's own self-attestation, the same discipline good open-source verification already applies to a company filing or an ownership record

The cost is small relative to the managed-services spend both surveys describe. The main decision is not technical: it is whether evidence generated on a loading dock, the kind I spent years generating myself, is admissible in the same conversation as evidence generated by a platform. None of the five actions above requires new headcount or a new system, only a field, a route and a short list that make existing evidence visible to the people who need it.

I have seen what this changes from the inside. On a Royal Navy supply chain account, physically locating equipment that had been recorded as lost let me close most of fourteen major loss investigations by recovery rather than write-off, and a recovered item closes without leaving a value gap in the account. The case study sets out how it was done.

Questions for the programme

  • Where does physical evidence enter our risk register today, and in which field?
  • When a goods-in or site-visit exception is raised, who outside operations ever hears about it?
  • Can the register tell a supplier that has never been physically checked from one that has failed a check three times this year?
  • Which Critical-tier suppliers have had an independent, in-person check in the last twelve months?

The check that goes unseen

The goods-in inspector will check the next delivery whether or not anyone names what the inspector is doing. That check happens thousands of times a day in every organisation with a physical supply chain, and today it usually ends where it started: resolved as an operational matter, and unseen by the risk function. A third-party risk programme that wants to know whether its supplier positions hold in practice already has a source of that evidence. Using it takes a field in the register for physical verification, a route for front-line exceptions into the same escalation path as any other finding, a short list of what counts as reportable, and independent in-person checks weighted towards the Critical tier. None of that needs a new department.

The question I would put to any TPRM programme is whether anyone with a view of the whole risk picture hears about the check the goods-in inspector has already made. The people closing the physical assurance gap are already in post. The programme's task is to make sure what they see reaches the register. KPMG's own two surveys made the case for that without meaning to, by describing everything the discipline is measuring except the evidence sitting closest to the ground.

The views expressed here are my own and are based on general observations about third-party risk management, physical assurance, and supply chain governance. They draw only on published sources and do not represent the position of the Royal Navy, the Ministry of Defence or any organisation with which I work. Nothing published here reproduces any organisation's internal policy, procedure or assurance output.
