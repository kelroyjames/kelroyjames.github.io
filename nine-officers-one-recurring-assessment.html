Nine Officers, One Recurring Assessment

Between 2014 and 2026, nine reporting officers across five different units, with no connection to each other, independently reached for the same word to describe the same thing. This is what the annual record actually shows, and what built it.

Kelroy James September 2026
📖 6 min read

Every serving member of the Royal Navy is assessed annually through a Service Judgement Appraisal Report, known as an SJAR: a first reporting officer's narrative, a second reporting officer's independent assessment, and a set of formal recommendations on promotion and future employment. I have thirteen of these on file going back to 2014, plus the current one covering April 2025 to March 2026. Read together rather than one at a time, they show something I had not noticed until I laid them side by side: a pattern that repeats across officers who never worked with each other, in units thousands of miles apart, years apart.

The Pattern

In 2014, at a Joint Forces Logistics Unit in the Falklands, my reporting officer wrote that he would welcome me to any future command, describing me as competent as any Leading Hand he had known. In 2016, at the European Joint Support Unit at SHAPE in Belgium, a reporting officer with 38 years of service placed me in the top ten Junior NCOs he had encountered in that time. In 2018, at Fleet Diving Squadron, my reporting officer wrote that I stood out as the most effective and proactive logistician within the wider Portsmouth flotilla, then repeated the same assessment in stronger terms six months later.

In 2019, at Fleet Operations, Commander Rob Cogan, whom I later thanked in my Aston University dissertation for his time as an interview respondent, wrote that I was one of the very best of the ten to twelve logistics specialists who had worked for him, and specifically that my BSc coursework had shown him my latent capability for higher rank. He wrote almost the same assessment again the following year, independently, without reference back to the first. In 2021, on HMS Defender, I was rated the strongest Leading Hand within the logistics department. Across the reports since, the language shifts slightly year to year, but the direction has not: each year's reporting officer, working from that year's evidence alone, arrives at a version of the same conclusion.

The most recent report, covering the year to March 2026, rates me A- overall, up from a B the year before, and recommends promotion as exceptional rather than developmental. The same report notes that I was assessed as ranking third among 79 peer senior rates across the wider Surface Flotilla headquarters, a comparison against the whole peer group rather than a single trade.

Personal Growth, Shaping Practice

What makes these reports useful rather than just flattering is what they credit as the reason. Cdr Cogan named my BSc coursework directly, twice, as evidence of capability beyond my rank at the time. The most recent report names the Defence Logistics Staff Course modules I have completed, and credits them for sharpening the judgement behind specific pieces of delivered work: an aide-memoire for validation checks, standardised loss-reporting packs, a Palantir Foundry application built with BAE Systems and DE&S for fleet-wide inventory interrogation, and the identification of £2.4M in misallocated expenditure that led to a proposed Navy-wide policy instruction. None of that is generic praise. Each item is something a specific report names, dates, and ties back to a specific piece of study.

Practice, Sharpening Delivery

The reports also show the study translating into delivery under people rather than on paper. Empowerment Day, the junior-rates development programme I founded, appears in the current report tied to measurable improvements in junior-rate progression and working environment. The financial control work that surfaced the £2.4M discrepancy is credited with restoring compliance and informing wider policy. The recommendation for promotion moved from developmental to exceptional in the same reporting cycle that these specific, dated pieces of delivery appear in the narrative. The pattern holds: personal growth shaped professional practice, and professional practice sharpens operational delivery.

The Public Version of the Same Thing

The clearest external confirmation of this pattern came two years after the fact, and voluntarily. Lieutenant Rob Jones, my reporting officer at Fleet Diving Squadron in 2018, wrote a public recommendation on LinkedIn in November 2020, with no professional reason to do so and no knowledge that I would ever compare it against his own SJAR wording from two years earlier:

"Of all the logistics specialists I worked with over 12 years if I could select one to support my engineers on the ground it would be Kelroy. His application of the defence procurement model and capability to network with all stakeholders to achieve the required outcome is second to none."

That is the same officer, years after any reporting obligation had ended, choosing on his own initiative to say in public what he had written privately at the time. Nine officers across a twelve-year career independently reaching the same conclusion is a pattern. One of them repeating it publicly, unprompted, years afterward, is corroboration.
