The Enterprise Risk Management We Already Do

Corporate risk management is being rewired around decisions, dependencies and judgement. Operational institutions have worked that way for decades, under other names.

Kelroy James September 2026
📖 8 min read 📊 1,620 words
Share on LinkedIn Share on X Share via Email

In August 2026, PwC UK published an insight paper titled "Rewiring Enterprise Risk Management." Its argument is that ERM has drifted into something process-led and backward-looking, in which risks are classified, reviewed on a cycle and reported through governance after the decisions that created them have already been taken. Its proposed correction rests on three shifts: starting with decisions rather than risk registers, preparing for how disruption actually moves rather than attempting to forecast every event, and using artificial intelligence to sharpen human judgement rather than to replace it.

I read it twice. Not because the argument was difficult, but because each of the three shifts described something I have watched operate at sea for eighteen years. It was simply never called enterprise risk management.

This is the third piece I have written on the same underlying theme. The first argued that accreditation tests a supplier's legitimacy on a given day. However, it does not test resilience, and the fragility tends to sit in the lower tiers where accreditation looks least closely. The second read the BAE Systems, Inc. settlement beyond its headline figure and found a regulator describing compliance failure at scale as substantially a failure of people, from which it follows that career paths and competence are themselves compliance controls. The pattern across all three is consistent: the frameworks that corporate risk management is now formalising describe practices that operational institutions have exercised for decades under different vocabulary. Those institutions did not develop them because a standard required it. They developed them because the consequences of not having them were immediate.

Risk at the point of decision

The first shift is the most consequential. The gap PwC identify is between where risk is created (in decisions, formal and informal) and where risk is reviewed, which is too often afterwards, on a reporting cycle, once momentum has become difficult to reverse. Their correction is to place risk expertise into the moments where choices are actually being made.

A warship cannot operate any other way. Command decisions at sea do not wait for a quarterly committee, and the risk judgement is exercised at the point of decision or it is not exercised at all. The operational planning process exists precisely to force assumptions into the open before commitment, and the brief-back, in which a subordinate states the plan and its risks back to the commander, is a challenge built into the decision rather than appended to it afterwards. In first-line assurance practice the same logic applies: a findings register that feeds decisions within the operating cycle, while the consequences remain cheap to change, is doing risk work. A register compiled for a report is doing something else.

The critique cuts both ways, however, and it would be dishonest to present it otherwise. Defence assurance also contains exactly the pattern PwC warn against: the periodic inspection that examines compliance after the fact, and the annual audit cycle that reports on decisions long since locked in. Consequently the lesson is not that Defence has nothing to learn here. It is that the decision-focused model already exists in the operational half of these institutions, and the work is to extend it into the assurance half.

Minimum viable is not a new idea at sea

The second shift introduces what PwC term the Minimum Viable Company: the smallest set of services, processes and dependencies that must continue working for an organisation to remain viable under severe stress. Rather than attempting to predict every disruption, leaders define what must be protected, preserved and restored when several things fail at once.

Every ship's company already carries this concept, because damage control depends upon it. When a ship takes damage nobody attempts to save everything simultaneously. The priorities are to float, to move and to fight, in that order, and every drill rehearses the dependencies beneath those three outcomes. Readiness states, mission-essential equipment schedules and minimum manning requirements are all expressions of the question PwC are now asking boards to answer. Having led a supply chain department through regeneration to readiness for sea, I would add that returning a warship to operational capability is precisely an exercise in rebuilding to a defined minimum viability and then beyond it, dependency by dependency, each tested under stress before it is trusted.

What strikes me most is the timing. Royal Navy policy direction put the minimum-viable question to its own bureaucracy at the start of this year, some months before corporate risk thinking arrived at the same lens, asking of compliance itself which checks genuinely protect safety and operational effectiveness and which persist because a process once demanded them. The two applications also presuppose one another. An organisation can only reduce its checking safely if it already knows which dependencies genuinely bear upon its viability. Conversely, once the minimum viable organisation has been defined, the natural next step is to reduce the assurance burden around everything outside it. One is the concept and the other is its implementation consequence, and on this occasion the operational institution reached the implementation first.

Human-led, AI-amplified

The third shift holds that AI should extend the reach and imagination of risk functions, detecting weak signals, testing assumptions and generating scenarios at pace, whilst the consequential judgements remain human, since those involve ambiguity, incomplete information and behaviour under pressure.

This is the settlement the maritime domain reached long ago. The sensor picture has grown enormously; the officer of the watch still decides. The value of AI in risk work is a wider field of view rather than the impression of automated certainty, and the assurance question which follows is the one I find most interesting: how does an organisation evidence that the tools amplifying its judgement are themselves trustworthy?

The gap here is measurable. EY's 2026 global technology executive survey reported that around a quarter of technology executives had enterprise-wide AI governance and ethical oversight in place for agentic AI, and roughly a third had ensured high-quality, accessible and governed data, at a point when more than three quarters expected AI investment to rise by at least twenty per cent over the following year. Capability is therefore scaling considerably faster than the governance wrapped around it. That is the space in which ISO/IEC 42001 and, in Defence, JSP 936 earn their place, since both exist to make trustworthiness demonstrable rather than assumed.

Behaviours rather than slogans

PwC close by asking risk functions to abandon vague talk of risk culture in favour of specific observable behaviours: whether leaders invite challenge early enough, whether weak signals are escalated, and whether assumptions are tested whilst there is still time to change course.

I would add one distinction to that, drawn from a year spent making this argument within the supply chain profession: the difference between compliance as behaviour and compliance as understanding. A checklist can be completed without being understood, and a unit can pass an inspection whilst holding no clear view of the risk the inspection exists to detect. A behaviour, by contrast, can be observed, coached and improved. Near-miss reporting, brief-backs and the willingness to let a junior rate stop a process they believe to be wrong are all behavioural interventions, and they long predate the language now used to describe them.

Underneath that sits a condition which no framework supplies. Assurance runs on data, and data is entered by people. Where an individual believes that reporting a problem honestly will cost them, the system receives a compliance performance rather than the truth, and every downstream analytic, every AI-derived insight and every credential resting on that record inherits the error. Psychological safety is therefore not a welfare consideration sitting alongside assurance. It is a precondition of it.

Two-way traffic

None of this claims that Defence has already solved what industry is still discovering. The honest conclusion is narrower. Corporate risk management is rewiring itself towards practices that institutions operating under immediate consequence developed from necessity, and the learning ought therefore to flow in both directions. Defence can adopt the discipline of naming, measuring and maturing what it currently does by instinct, since a practice which is never named is never taught, never assured and never improved deliberately. Industry, in turn, can study institutions in which risk management was never optional and in which the feedback loop was rarely longer than a watch.

The vocabulary matters more than it appears to. Practitioners who cannot name what they are doing cannot defend it, transfer it, or recognise it when a consultancy sells it back to them under a different label. That, more than any individual framework, is what I think the convergence is really telling us.
