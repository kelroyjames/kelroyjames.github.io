Study Shapes Practice, Practice Shapes Delivery

A career spent studying alongside serving, and a habit that keeps showing up the same way: what I learn turns into what I deliver. This essay sets out what that looks like from the outside, and why the Service should care about it as a retention lever.

Kelroy James September 2026
📖 2 min read

Across eighteen years of service, in postings spanning a Falklands logistics role, a NATO staff appointment, a mine-warfare squadron and a front-line warship, I have kept a consistent habit: study alongside the job. Appraisal content is not mine to publish, so this essay describes what the habit produced, which can be checked without reference to any report.

What the study produced

Formal study has tracked onto specific, delivered outcomes, each dated and each traceable to something concrete:

  • An aide-memoire for validation checks, built to close a gap in how discrepancies were caught.
  • Standardised loss-reporting packs, replacing ad hoc reporting with a repeatable format.
  • A rebuilt inventory-tracking spreadsheet feeding the fleet's data platform.
  • The identification of £2.4M in misallocated expenditure, which led to a proposed Navy-wide policy instruction, set out in full in the case study on this site.
  • Empowerment Day, a junior-rates development programme I founded, with measurable effects on junior-rate progression and working environment.

Each of these exists, was implemented, and can be checked against what it changed.

In public, unprompted

Formal appraisal is Official-Sensitive and stays that way, so this essay relies on what people have chosen to say publicly on their own initiative. Rob Jones, an officer I served under in 2018, wrote on LinkedIn in November 2020, two years after we worked together and with no professional reason to:

"Of all the logistics specialists I worked with over 12 years if I could select one to support my engineers on the ground it would be Kelroy."

Rob Jones CEng MIET FCMI

Project Manager at Thales · Officer in Charge, Royal Navy unit (2018)

That is a single data point and I am treating it as one. It carries weight because it came years after any reporting relationship had ended, with nothing riding on it.

Beyond one career

Opportunities for personal development (48%) and for career development (46%) are now among the factors keeping regular personnel in the Service, both up on the year before, according to the Ministry of Defence's 2026 Armed Forces Continuous Attitude Survey, which drew on a sample of over 32,000 trained regulars (Ministry of Defence, 2026). Royal Navy personnel reported lower satisfaction with professional development opportunities than the Army or RAF, narrowly ahead of only the Royal Marines (Ministry of Defence, 2026).

Set against a career in which study repeatedly turned into delivered, checkable outcomes, that satisfaction gap looks like an opportunity the Service has not yet used. In my view, a retention lever that already produces results does not need to be invented. It needs to be resourced and pointed at more people, so that the habit described here stops being a personal one.
