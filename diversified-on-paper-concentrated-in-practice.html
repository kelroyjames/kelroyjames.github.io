Diversified on Paper, Concentrated in Practice

A supplier list with fifty names on it can still fail from a single point. This piece sets out how to measure the concentration that headcount hides, and what to do once it has been found.

Kelroy James Updated September 2026
📖 8 min read 📊 1,437 words
Share on LinkedIn (opens in a new tab) Share on X (opens in a new tab) Share via Email

In March 2000, a fire at a Philips semiconductor plant in Albuquerque, New Mexico, damaged a cleanroom that supplied radio-frequency chips to two of the world's largest phone makers. Both Ericsson and Nokia bought from the same production line. Ericsson treated the plant as a reliable single source and waited for Philips's own assessment before acting, while Nokia had a second qualified source already running and an escalation process that put engineers on the ground within days. Nokia lost weeks of margin. Ericsson lost an estimated few hundred million dollars in unfilled handset orders and exited the phone manufacturing business not long after, and Norrman and Jansson (2004) document the proactive supply chain risk management approach it adopted once the accident had exposed the vulnerability. The fire was the trigger, but the concentration of one critical input inside a single, untested relationship appears to have been present in Ericsson's supplier list well before it.

On 16 September 2025, in a more recent case with the same pattern, a fire damaged the hot mill at Novelis's plant in Oswego, New York, which reportedly supplies about 40% of the aluminium sheet the US automotive industry uses. A second fire followed on 20 November, and the mill restarted only in June 2026. Ford reported a $2 billion headwind and said it would keep sourcing finished materials from the plant and from other suppliers. Both cases raise the question this essay examines: how can an organisation tell whether a supplier base that looks diversified is in fact concentrated where it matters? The discussion considers why a supplier count may mislead, how concentration can be measured, what the measure cannot show, and what options follow when it comes back high.

The vanity metric

ISO 31000 (2018) defines risk as the effect of uncertainty on objectives, which implies that concentration matters only where a supplier's failure would affect an objective. Asked against that definition, "How many suppliers do we have?" appears to be a vanity metric. An organisation can hold two hundred active suppliers and still be one plant fire away from a crisis, because two hundred is a count across the whole book, and risk does not distribute evenly across a book. It tends to concentrate inside whichever category is both critical and thin. A distribution business might have forty suppliers of packaging materials and exactly one supplier of the specialist component that goes into its highest-margin product line, and the forty would reveal nothing useful about the one that matters.

COSO's enterprise risk management framework (2017) integrates risk with strategy and performance, which suggests a more useful question: of the spend or dependency that would actually hurt if it stopped, how much sits with how few hands? That question may need asking separately for each risk tier, because concentration inside a Critical tier is a different conversation from concentration inside Low. A Critical tier concentrated in one supplier is a single point of failure sitting where it can do the most damage, while the same concentration inside Low may simply be an efficient procurement decision. It follows that the tier in which concentration sits, as much as the concentration figure itself, decides whether it needs action.

A number that can actually be calculated

The US Department of Justice and Federal Trade Commission measure market concentration with the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI): each player's share of a market is squared and the results are summed. A market with one player scores 10,000, and a market with a hundred equal players scores 100. The agencies' Horizontal Merger Guidelines (2010) treated a market above 2,500 as highly concentrated, and their Merger Guidelines (2023) lowered that threshold, treating a market above 1,800 as highly concentrated. The same formula can be applied internally, one risk tier at a time: each supplier's share of spend or dependency within the tier is squared, and the squares are summed. Run tier by tier, the score cannot be flattered by forty packaging suppliers hiding one sole-source component, so it may show whether "diversified" is true for the tier that matters or only true on average.

Worked example (illustrative)

Three suppliers sit in the Critical tier of a fictional distribution company's risk register: a sole-source component supplier, an IT hosting provider, and a specialist subcontractor. Their share of Critical-tier spend:

Worked example of a concentration score: each supplier's share of Critical spend, and that share squared.
SupplierShare of Critical spendShare²
Sole-source component supplier45%2,025
IT hosting provider35%1,225
Specialist subcontractor20%400

HHI = 2,025 + 1,225 + 400 = 3,650

3,650 → highly concentrated (above both the 2010 threshold of 2,500 and the 2023 threshold of 1,800)

In the example, two of those three suppliers, the hosting provider and the subcontractor, were already carrying open, escalated findings on the risk register. A high HHI may explain why problems at individual suppliers in that tier land harder than they would in a genuinely diversified one, since there is little slack in the tier to absorb a single failure.

What the number does not show

Ford's statement that it would keep sourcing from Novelis, alongside other suppliers, suggests that concentration is not always abandoned even after it fails. Concentration can be the right call: a single strategic partner might offer better quality control, tighter protection of intellectual property, or economies of scale that a fragmented supplier base cannot match. An HHI score is therefore best read as a trigger for a conversation. Its value lies in making concentration a decision someone actually made, with the risk understood, instead of something that accumulated silently because nobody was measuring it. The failure it guards against is undiscovered concentration, discovered for the first time when the single point actually fails.

The same options Nokia had

Nokia's response in 2000 suggests the options available once a Critical-tier HHI comes back high, and they are the same tools that sit at Stage 8, exit or replacement planning, of a properly run third-party risk lifecycle:

  • Qualify a warm second source even if it never takes live volume, so the requalification clock has already been run before it is needed, not after
  • Size safety stock to the requalification lead time of the concentrated input, not to average demand variability, since that is the specific gap a concentration failure creates
  • Diversify geography or jurisdiction where the concentration risk is a single site or a single regulatory regime
  • Negotiate step-in rights and IP escrow so a sole-source failure does not also mean losing the ability to make or specify the item
  • Revisit the classification itself: if a Critical-tier input has been single-sourced for years without incident, that is worth checking against Stage 2 of the lifecycle

Each of these steps can be taken while keeping a good supplier relationship, since none requires ending the relationship that created the concentration. What they appear to require is knowing, in a number, that the concentration exists before a fire, a shortage or a regulatory change forces the discovery. Nokia's second source cost money to keep qualified and running for years before it was ever needed, which may be the price of not finding out the hard way.

Before the fire, not after

  • What is the concentration score for each Critical-tier category, and who owns the number?
  • Which sole-source or near sole-source inputs have run for years without incident, and when were they last reclassified?
  • How long would requalifying a second source take, and does safety stock cover that period?
  • Is the concentration a single company, or also a single site, geography or regulatory regime?

The arithmetic sitting unread

Drawing these arguments together, the answer to the question appears to be that diversification can be tested only tier by tier, and that a supplier count on its own may conceal the concentration that matters. Ericsson's exposure came down to one number nobody had calculated, how much of a critical input sat inside a single relationship at a single site, while Nokia had in effect priced the same risk into the second source it kept running. The Herfindahl-Hirschman Index offers a way to turn that difference into a number before the fire. It does not call for eliminating concentration, which is sometimes the right commercial choice, and its value lies in showing whether concentration in a Critical tier was a decision someone made or a position that accumulated while nobody was counting. Novelis and Ford suggest the same exposure still plays out, on a different continent and in a different material, and the arithmetic that would flag it is likely to be sitting unread in most organisations' own supplier data.
