Talent Should Flow Both Ways

The Pan-Defence Skills Framework as a Defence Industrial Strategy tool, connecting service leavers to the industrial base that needs their exact skills.

Kelroy James 2026
📖 7 min read 📊 1,642 words
Share on LinkedIn (opens in a new tab) Share on X (opens in a new tab) Share via Email

Regular Armed Forces numbers are down 8% since 2022. The ambition is to grow the Reserves by 20%. The defence industrial base is expanding. None of that works if experienced professionals have to enter at the bottom. The MOD is already piloting an alternative, and most people have not noticed.

Most discussion of Defence skills frameworks focuses on getting people out well. The more strategically interesting question is how we get experienced people in, and how we keep moving them between the Ministry of Defence, the defence industry and the Reserves without treating each move as a fresh start. The UK now has both the strategic driver and the technical mechanism to do this, but they are not yet fully connected. Closing that gap, between the ambition and the join-up, is the actual work.

The driver

The 2025 Defence Industrial Strategy is unambiguous about the workforce challenge. It commits £182 million to strengthen the future skills of the workforce, and states plainly that Government will make it easier and faster for members of the Armed Forces and workers in other industries to move into the defence sector. Critically, it also commits to making it easier to transfer into and across defence mid-career by scoping the development of a Defence Skills Passport. That passport is intended to build on the Ministry of Defence's industry-benchmarked Pan-Defence skills work and on ZigZag Careers, creating a digital record of an individual's skills that enables smoother identification and transfer between the Armed Forces, defence industry and those looking to enter from neighbouring industries.

The pressure behind this is real. Analysis of the Strategic Defence Review notes that Regular Armed Forces numbers declined by 8% since 2022, that shortages now affect military, civil service and industrial roles across manufacturing, engineering and advanced technology, and that Defence supports over 239,000 jobs. The Review also commits to boosting Reserve numbers by 20% when funding allows, alongside a whole force, skills-based approach. Defence cannot expand the Reserves by a fifth, staff a growing industrial base and simultaneously insist that experienced professionals begin at the bottom of a rank ladder.

The mechanism

The Pan-Defence Skills Framework already provides the architecture. It was designed as a Whole Force approach covering Regulars, Reservists and civil servants, giving Defence a single common language for skills, knowledge, experience and behaviours, with individual validated skills profiles held digitally. Two features of that design matter enormously for talent mobility. The first is what the framework captures, and the second is what it deliberately lets go of, and each does different work in making lateral entry possible.

First, it captures skills acquired outside Defence. Personnel are encouraged to log capability from their civilian lives, from language proficiency to professional qualifications, which was previously either passed by word of mouth or lost entirely. Senior officers leading the rollout have highlighted Reservists specifically, noting that they hold a wealth of experience from outside Defence that is potentially very valuable. That capture matters because it turns tacit knowledge nobody officially tracked into a searchable record the whole enterprise can draw on.

Second, it decouples capability from rank as the primary organising principle. Defence has openly discussed employing people according to the skillset required, rather than treating rank as a proxy for what someone can actually do. That is the foundation for genuine lateral entry: someone with the right expertise no longer has to hold the rank a role was historically built around before they can be considered for it. Without that shift, capturing outside skills in a database changes nothing, because rank would still gate who gets to use them.

It is already being piloted

The ZigZag Career Pathway already exists: a skills-focused pilot designed to bring talented professionals into the Armed Forces in fixed-term roles that match their expertise, explicitly breaking away from the conventional military career ladder. It is open to civilians with no prior service, to veterans returning, and to Reservists seeking a more flexible contribution, with minimal military training required and a tailored induction. The language on entry level is direct: for veterans and current reservists, the pathway offers roles that reflect industry experience rather than previous rank, so that individuals can re-engage at an appropriate level and make the most of hard-earned skills. Advertised ZigZag roles have included cyber threat intelligence and digital support posts at specified grades, which is a working proof of concept for capability-based entry rather than a paper commitment.

The strategic case for making this systematic

Treat the framework as an enterprise-wide talent market rather than an internal HR system, and several things become possible. Defence can buy scarce expertise it cannot grow at pace. Cyber, data, AI assurance, systems engineering, nuclear skills, trade compliance and complex programme delivery are contested across the whole economy. Sourcing a mid-career specialist through a Reserve or fixed-term route at a level proportionate to their capability is faster than training one for a decade.

Industry gains a legible talent pool. At present a defence prime reading a Service CV is performing an act of interpretation. A validated, industry-benchmarked skills record removes that friction and reduces the risk premium employers apply to military candidates. The employer no longer has to guess what a candidate can do, because the framework has already done the vouching.

Retention improves. People are more likely to stay in an enterprise where they can move without penalty than one where every transition costs them status. A skills passport makes a career that moves between MOD, industry and the Reserves a rational choice rather than a gamble. That matters directly to the 20% Reserve growth target: a passport removes one of the real reasons people leave and don't come back.

Assurance and oversight capability improves on both sides. Someone who has operated first-line controls inside Defence, then worked in commercial supply chain governance or export control, then returned as a Reservist, understands both the regulator's expectation and the operator's reality. That combination is rare and increasingly valuable given the enforcement risk now attached to export control and supply chain compliance in the sector. A framework that lets that person move freely between the two sides, rather than starting over each time, is what makes that rare combination available to draw on at all.

Sovereign capability becomes less fragile. Skills held by one organisation and invisible to the rest of the enterprise are single points of failure. A framework that makes those skills visible across MOD, industry and the Reserves turns a single point of failure into shared capacity the whole enterprise can call on. Five separate benefits, all resting on the same underlying fix: that is the strategic case in full.

What needs to be true for this to work

Two things here deserve honest treatment. First, the Defence Skills Passport is being scoped, not delivered. The strategy commits to scoping an initial framework, operating concept and pilot opportunities through sector-wide consultation supported by industry early adopters. That is the right start, but scoping is not implementation, and the value only appears when industry recognises the record as authoritative.

Second, capability-based entry is not a route around standards. Professional registration, security clearance, competence assurance and safety qualification requirements exist for good reasons and should be met in full. The argument is narrower and more defensible: prior demonstrated capability should be assessed before anyone is treated as a beginner. Getting that sequencing right, assessment before entry rather than exemption from entry requirements, is the difference between broadening the pipeline and lowering the bar.

That distinction matters, because conflating the two is exactly what makes people distrust capability-based entry in the first place. Recognition of prior capability is a measure of accuracy: it credits what someone can already demonstrably do, rather than assuming a blank slate because the demonstration happened outside a defence uniform. My own attempt at that accuracy, mapping published Royal Navy role descriptions against civilian control frameworks, is set out in Independently Verified Skills Mapping. It is the same exercise the Defence Skills Passport will eventually need to run at scale, for every trade, not just my own.

The question to ask

Defence has historically asked what skills it needs and how to train them. The better question is how Defence makes skills visible, portable and recognisable as people move between Service, industry and the wider economy, in both directions and more than once. The Pan-Defence Skills Framework, the ZigZag pilot and the proposed Defence Skills Passport are three parts of the same answer. They currently sit in different places, owned by different parts of the enterprise, with different levels of maturity.

Joining them up is a decision about whether we regard a career as a ladder or as a network, not a technology problem: the framework, the pilot and the passport already exist in some form. Given the scale of the recruitment, retention and industrial capacity challenge in front of us, a network looks considerably more useful than a ladder that only runs one way. The 8% decline and the 20% Reserve ambition set out at the start of this piece are not solved by recruiting harder into the same rank-based entry point. They are solved by making it normal for talent to move in, out, sideways and back again, and by treating each of those moves as evidence of capability rather than a fresh start.
