Protecting a £5M Trial from Failure

Turning round a rejected consignment in three days to keep a £5M weapons trial on schedule, then recovering critical equipment mid-exercise in Norway with three hours to spare.

The Challenge

Two Wildcat Maritime Force deployments, a year apart, shared the same underlying problem. Programme-critical trials and exercises do not wait for logistics to catch up. In both cases a late-stage complication threatened equipment that had to be in a specific place on a specific date, and in both cases the fix had to be found in days, then hours, not weeks.

A Rejected Consignment

On joining Wildcat Maritime Force, I took over responsibility for the logistics movements running from the UK to a forward support location, supporting a weapons integration trial recognised at programme level as a critically important enterprise success, and one I understood to be valued at around £5 million.

The team had just had a consignment rejected. With the next flight slot fixed and the trial's flying programme unable to absorb a slip, I had three days to turn the inventory and the paperwork around, correct what had caused the rejection, and get the consignment cleared in time to make that flight. Once in post, I coordinated the movement through to a successful conclusion, and the support arrangement went on to sustain the trial for its duration.

The Real Constraint

A rejected consignment doesn't get a second attempt on the same schedule. The trial's flying programme was fixed long before the equipment was; the only variable that could still move was how fast the paperwork and the physical stock were made to agree.

Seven Containers to Norway

Exercise Tamber Shield is an annual, UK-led exercise run under the Joint Expeditionary Force and hosted by Norway, built around Royal Navy Wildcat helicopters working alongside Norwegian patrol vessels and warships in the fjords near Bergen. For Wildcat Maritime Force, taking part meant packing and moving seven containers of equipment across borders to support the detachment.

I coordinated the packing consignment and the cross-border movement of all seven containers, then deployed to theatre myself to oversee the return leg. While there, a critical piece of equipment was pulled from the aircraft to meet a more urgent requirement from a front-line unit, a reasonable call operationally, but one that left the detachment without kit it needed to complete the exercise and return on schedule.

I raised the demand, and coordinated the issue, despatch and customs clearance of the replacement equipment against the deadline of the return flight. Both the aircraft's return and the final container delivery closed with three hours to spare.

Recovering Mid-Exercise

Losing a critical item mid-exercise is a different problem from losing one before deploying. There's no lead time left to absorb it, and the fix has to run in parallel with everything else already happening: the demand, the clearance and the delivery all had to land inside the same closing window as the return flight itself.

Why This Mattered

Both movements sat directly upstream of trials and exercises that could not simply wait:

  • Trial schedules are fixed months in advance – Ship and range time, aircraft availability and multiple duty-holding chains are booked well ahead; a logistics delay can force a trial to be re-sought from scratch, not just a slipped date
  • A single aircraft carries disproportionate risk – Both the weapons trial and the Norway detachment ran on a small number of airframes, so equipment gaps translate directly into lost flying hours that are expensive and slow to recover
  • Multinational exercises run on trust – A UK detachment that can't sustain its own equipment supply reflects on the wider Joint Expeditionary Force partnership, not just the unit

In both cases, the equipment problem was really a schedule problem wearing a logistics disguise, and the fix had to be measured in the same units as the deadline: days, then hours.

The Scale That Depended on It

The weapons trial that the consignment recovery kept on schedule went on to be recognised at programme level as a critically important enterprise success, run against a fixed flying programme on a small number of airframes. None of that activity happens if the equipment doesn't clear customs in time to make the flight. A missed flight slot doesn't just cost three days: trial windows of that size are booked months out and can't simply be rebooked, so the three-day fix was protecting weeks of trial time, not just the immediate consignment.

Lessons Learned

A Rejection Runs on the Trial's Deadline

When a consignment is rejected against a fixed flight or trial date, the response time is set by the schedule, not by how much correction is actually needed. Three days was what the flight allowed, so three days was what the fix had to take.

Plan for the Equipment to Move Again After It Arrives

Equipment delivered for one purpose can be reallocated to a more urgent one mid-exercise, for entirely reasonable operational reasons. A movements plan that only accounts for the outbound leg misses exactly the kind of recovery that ends up mattering most.

Being in Theatre Changes What You Can Fix

Coordinating an outbound movement from the UK and coordinating a recovery from inside the exercise are different jobs. Deploying to oversee the return leg meant the demand, issue and clearance could be run in parallel by one person holding the whole picture, rather than handed across a time zone at the point it mattered most.

Margin Is a Design Choice

Three hours of margin on a return flight and a container delivery is what is left over when every step in a recovery chain, such as demand, issue, despatch and clearance, runs in parallel rather than in sequence: a design choice, not an accident.

A Vision Statement Builds Ownership

The Mission Model Canvas training was a serial within a strategic-innovation programme I was completing myself, and I used it to set out a vision statement for the team rather than a list of instructions. Explaining why the work mattered, alongside what needed to be done, gave people a stake in the outcome rather than just a task to complete. I trace the growth mindset behind six of the nine going on to be promoted back to that shift in ownership.

Civilian Relevance

Deadline-critical coordination and multi-agency liaison under a fixed external schedule: the same pressure that defines regulated trial, launch or compliance-deadline management in industry, where the date moves for no one and the fix has to run in parallel, not in sequence.
