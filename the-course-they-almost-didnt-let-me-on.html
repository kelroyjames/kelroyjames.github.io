The Course They Almost Didn't Let Me On

The Defence Logistics Staff Course is built for high calibre officers of SO2/3 rank, following completion of their single-service career development staff courses. I applied as a Petty Officer. This is what got me a place on it, and what five modules against real defence procurement problems have actually taught me so far.

Kelroy James September 2026
📖 8 min read

What does it take for a Petty Officer to earn a place on a staff course built for officers? For me it took most of three years of groundwork I had not planned as an application. I had been working through MIT's Supply Chain Management MicroMasters in the way I described in an earlier piece: properly for a few weeks, then not at all for a few months, until it became a serious habit. As my resettlement window came closer, I needed to use the time left differently. The Defence Logistics Staff Course was funded, structured and ran to a timetable I did not set myself, so I gave it the same priority as the MicroMasters. Making the case for a place on it turned out to be most of the work.

A Course Built for a Different Rank

The DLSC awards a Post Graduate Diploma in Logistics Management, extendable to a full MSc through a self-funded dissertation. It is delivered through a partnership between the Logistics Management Training Squadron at RAF College Cranwell and the University of Lincoln. Its governing instruction is specific about who it is for: "high calibre officers of SO2/3 rank from the Royal Navy, Army, Royal Marines and Royal Air Force (Logistics and Engineer Branches), ideally following completion of their single service career development staff courses," along with Civil Service logisticians at Band C2 and above. Petty Officer sits outside that cohort. The same instruction sets out a separate route for Warrant Officers and SNCOs, considered case by case on previous logistics and academic experience, clearly articulated motivation and how the course would benefit Defence.

My application leaned on the MIT coursework directly. The MicroMasters and an earlier MIT-affiliated certificate in advanced supply chain systems planning and design sat in my academic history alongside a foundation degree, a bachelor's degree and prior Defence logistics training. My line manager's supporting statement rested on work I had already delivered. I was selected for Cohort 14, which runs from April 2025 to March 2027.

Five Modules Against Five Real Problems

The DLSC has eight modules: six compulsory, delivered first, and two electives from a pool of six. Each is a one-week residential period at Cranwell followed by an assignment marked to Masters-level criteria by the University of Lincoln. I have completed the six compulsory modules bar the final capstone, and my marks so far are these.

The marks matter less to me than what each assignment made me do: take the module's theory and point it at a live problem inside Defence that I already knew from the inside. Nobody handed me a case study written for someone else's industry, and the marks followed from that.

Empowerment Day, Under a Change-Management Lens

For Organisational Leadership and Change, I used Empowerment Day, the junior-rates development programme I founded within Engineering Support Group 1, as the case study. I applied Lewin's Unfreeze-Change-Refreeze framework, Kotter's Eight-Step Change Model and Hiatt's ADKAR model to what had until then been a practical initiative with no theory behind it. The analysis drew on published data: the RN/RM voluntary outflow rate stood at 5.9% in the year to March 2024, and that year's Armed Forces Continuous Attitude Survey found 26% of personnel cited a lack of career development opportunities, and 24% a lack of personal development opportunities, among their reasons for considering leaving. My own anonymised survey of the pilot found 68% of junior rates had felt overlooked before it. The recommendations went further than the pilot: standing forums feeding junior-rate input into unit planning, inclusive leadership built into promotion and appraisal criteria, and links to the Royal Navy's Culture Blueprint and Armed Forces People Strategy so the work outlasts one enthusiastic sponsor. Empowerment Day ran again in December 2025 and in July 2026, with management endorsement to continue as a recurring event.

Project Selborne and the Culture Problem Underneath It

Decision Support in Logistics let me treat Project Selborne, the Royal Navy's training modularisation programme, as a decision-making problem. Drawing on Kahneman's distinction between fast and deliberate thinking, the Cynefin framework's treatment of complex and merely complicated problems, and the Nominal Group Technique for reducing groupthink in hierarchies, the assignment argued that the programme's biggest obstacle is cultural: evidence needs to be weighed on its merits, whoever raises it. The recommendations were cross-disciplinary review across the training pipeline and enterprise-wide data standardisation through platforms such as the Defence Learning and Management Capability, so training data is usable by the people who need it. The fix was a habit of weighing evidence on its merits, built into how the programme makes decisions, and it needed no new technology.

A Lower-Tier Supplier, Properly Checked

Procurement gave me the clearest link to work already on this site. The assignment was a strategic procurement appraisal of a defence prime's lower-tier supply chain, applying an open-source due-diligence method to a representative Tier 3 supplier with the Kraljic Matrix, the CIPS procurement cycle and the 10Cs supplier evaluation framework. Its main contribution was an integrity-led due-diligence layer for lower-tier defence suppliers: formal OSINT screening for critical suppliers, collaborative assurance support for the SMEs a prime depends on, and governance segmented by each supplier's effect on capability. It is the same discipline behind the OSINT Supplier Verification framework.

What the Reverse Supply Chain Is Costing Defence

Logistics and Supply Chain Management compared the Ministry of Defence's reverse supply chain, recovering, repairing and redeploying equipment, against reverse logistics in the automotive sector, using the 4Rs model and Level of Repair Analysis. Grounded in National Audit Office reporting on the MoD's global supply chain and support information systems, it argued that Defence is weakest on the dimensions the automotive sector has already solved: data visibility, supplier collaboration and economic repair-or-replace decisions. The recommendations were concrete. Key suppliers would move from short-term transactional contracts to five-to-ten-year performance-based agreements, piloted with the top ten. Level of Repair Analysis would start on one high-value platform to prove the data before scaling. A dedicated analysis cell of fifty to a hundred trained MoD and industry personnel would run it.

Strategic Management, the fifth module, was assessed against a commercial case study. It applied the Strategy Diamond, PESTLE, Porter's Five Forces and VRIO to the same habit of never taking a stated strategy at face value until it has been tested against evidence. Working the frameworks against an unfamiliar business made that habit clearer, because there was no institutional familiarity to lean on, and showed me it transfers outside Defence.

Where I Am Now

I am working through Financing Defence Logistics, the first of my two electives, which introduces financial and management accounting for logistics decisions. It is assessed by a 4,000-word report on a constructed defence support company, covering cash management, investment appraisal, cost-volume-profit analysis, pricing, sources of capital and ratio analysis. I expected it to be the hardest module, and so far it is. On a real programme the numbers under a procurement decision are usually somebody else's job, and this is the first time I have had to build them myself.

My second elective is Responsible Supply Chain Strategy, added to the pool after the University of Lincoln won a competitive retender between January and April 2026. The new five-year contract opens a fresh cycle of cohorts from May 2026 to September 2032, while my Cohort 14 finishes on the existing contract with Module 8 in March 2027. The module, informed by the Institute of Environmental Management and Assessment and the International Sustainability and ESG Practitioners body, is assessed through a Business Implementation Plan on sustainability and ethics in a supply chain, the elective closest to my GRC and assurance work. After that comes the capstone, a work-based project negotiated with a supervisor and the Dissertation Coordinator on a real issue.

The Post Graduate Diploma carries accreditation from the Chartered Institute of Logistics and Transport, the Chartered Institute of Procurement and Supply and the Chartered Management Institute, which makes it legible outside Defence as well as inside it. The MSc is a separate, self-funded step. The PGDip attracts an eighteen-month Return of Service, a reasonable trade for a funded Masters-level qualification I would not otherwise have reached.

Neither Replaces the Other

The MIT coursework taught me to reason formally about supply chains as physical, financial and information flows between trading partners. The DLSC is teaching me to apply that reasoning inside Defence procurement, MOD funding structures and the accountability chains that come with spending public money on equipment that has to work the first time it is used in anger. The MicroMasters gave me the general theory, and the DLSC is where I am testing it against real procurement problems and a junior-rates programme that had to work in practice.

The course was built for a higher rank than mine, so my case for a place had to be made on paper, against evidence. It rested on coursework already completed and a line manager's statement about delivered work, and the case-by-case discretion clause gave the selection panel room to weigh it. The five modules above are my evidence that the discretion was exercised well.
