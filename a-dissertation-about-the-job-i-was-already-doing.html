A Dissertation About the Job I Was Already Doing

Between 2017 and 2019, while working in Fleet Operations at Northwood, I completed a BSc (Hons) in Logistics and Operations Management at Aston University. The final year dissertation was built around the exact problem I was already responsible for at work: forecasting fuel for the fleet.

Kelroy James September 2026
📖 6 min read

Most of what I have written on this site so far covers 2023 onward: the MIT MicroMasters, the Defence Logistics Staff Course, the case studies drawn from operational assurance work. There is a piece missing from that timeline. Between 2017 and 2019, while posted to the Fleet Operations division at Northwood Headquarters, I completed a BSc (Hons) in Logistics and Operations Management at Aston University, studying part-time alongside the job. It sits behind everything else on this site rather than in front of it, and it deserves the same treatment as the rest.

Three Years, Alongside the Job

The degree was delivered through Aston University's School of Engineering and Applied Science, taken part-time over three years while I remained in post at Northwood. Six taught modules ran across the first two years, followed by a final year dissertation module, EC310A. I graduated with a 2:1.

What made the degree worth doing properly, rather than just completing, was how directly the coursework overlapped with the work in front of me. I was studying logistics from inside a Fleet Operations department already responsible for exactly the kind of planning the modules described.

Six Modules, Building Toward the Same Argument

Each module produced a substantial written assignment, and looking back at them together shows a consistent thread rather than six unrelated pieces of coursework.

Multi-Modal Transport Management examined a proposed modal shift of container transport within the UK, comparing unimodal and multimodal networks and the case for intermodal solutions. Inventory Management used a manufacturing case study to work through operations strategy, line sequencing, and the comparative value of vendor-managed versus customer-managed inventory. Sustainability in Logistics Activities looked at reverse logistics and closed-loop supply chains in a military context, the process the armed forces call retrograde: recovering, repairing and redistributing assets rather than replacing them.

Lean, Agile and Competitiveness critiqued lean and agile approaches to supply chain management across different business sectors. Leadership and Human Resources Management, taken in the final year, examined organisational resistance to change through Lewin's and Kotter's models. Financing the Supply Chain, also final year, covered the Defence Acquisition Trade Cycle: strategic procurement, Smart Acquisition and Through-Life Support, Contracting for Availability, integrated logistics support, and how each is measured.

Two of those six modules turned out to be a first draft of arguments I would return to years later. The reverse logistics assignment is, in substance, the same question I examined again in 2025 on the Defence Logistics Staff Course, comparing the MoD's reverse supply chain against automotive sector practice. The Defence Acquisition Trade Cycle assignment covers much of the same ground as the Financing Defence Logistics and Procurement Management modules I am studying now. The questions have followed me for seven years.

The Dissertation: Forecasting Fuel for a Fleet

The final year project examined the forecasting of fuel consumption for Royal Navy ships, supervised by David J. Carpenter, at a point where fuel accounted for tens of millions of pounds of the RN's annual operating budget. I chose Action Research as the methodology, following the framework Saunders, Lewis and Thornhill describe as the Research Onion, specifically because I held a direct operational role in the department the research examined, and because the topic had been proposed by my own line management as an area where they wanted innovative thinking.

Primary data came from semi-structured, open-ended questionnaires with senior stakeholders directly responsible for fuel forecasting and data management, gathered under a formal informed-consent process through Aston's ethics guidelines. I am grateful to Commander Rob Cogan of the Maritime Operations Centre, Fleet Operations division at Northwood, Dr Charles Alpass and the staff of the Maritime Warfare Centre at HMS Collingwood, and Lieutenant Commander Francis Griffiths for their time and expertise. Secondary research drew on National Audit Office reports into the MoD's supply chain and financial management, and the Ministry of Defence's Joint Concept Note on human-machine teaming.

The findings were consistent with what the National Audit Office had already found at organisational level: fuel forecasting depended on a manual reporting process rather than any electronic or real-time data source, with no central repository, meaning strategic budgeting, operational planning and procurement teams each worked from separately maintained copies of the same underlying figures. Forecast error translated directly into cost, either as in-year underspend that could have been allocated elsewhere, or as the need to source additional fuel at short notice and at a price premium.

The recommendation was a single, integrated, service-oriented data architecture to replace the patchwork of legacy systems, positioning the department to make use of the machine learning and business intelligence capability the Navy was already exploring through its Programme Nelson initiative. Read now, it is an early and fuel-specific version of an argument I keep making: that forecasts, decisions and assurance are only as reliable as the data feeding them, and that the data problem is usually the real problem hiding underneath the process problem.

What Carried Forward

This degree is the piece that was missing from the published account of 2017 to 2019: a Bachelor's degree completed part-time, examined against a real operational problem before it was ever examined by Aston, and built on the same argument about data and decision-making that runs through everything I have written since. The Defence Logistics Staff Course gave that argument a second, more senior audience, years after a dissertation had already made the case.
