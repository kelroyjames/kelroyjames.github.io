Supply Chain Management Was Already a Discipline

Five MIT courses gave a name to work I had been doing for years without one. The Ministry of Defence has just signed a research partnership with the institution's UK research centre, which makes this a reasonable moment to explain what the coursework actually contains, and why another serving logistician should not wait for a formal pathway before starting it.

Kelroy James September 2026
📖 6 min read

I am reading through the key concepts document for MIT's Supply Chain Management MicroMasters ahead of a comprehensive final exam in November: one hundred and fifty minutes, a single sitting, covering all five courses at once. The coursework itself is done. The exam will come and go in a few hours in November, and a question the reading keeps raising will still be sitting there afterwards: why none of this felt like new information the first time I met it, and why it changed how I think about the job anyway.

Before the partnership existed

I first came across this course around 2016, on a NATO staff appointment at Supreme Headquarters Allied Powers Europe. EdX was still a fairly new platform at that point, and a Royal Navy stores account is not the obvious place to be browsing it, but the syllabus looked exactly like the framework I had been missing without knowing it had a name. I worked through some of the material that tour, the way most people work through a course on the side of a full-time job: properly for a few weeks, then not at all for a few months. It took until 2023, in a completely different job, to sit down and actually pass the five courses one at a time as a verified learner rather than someone reading along. The comprehensive exam is the last piece, a decade after I first opened the material and three years after I started taking it seriously.

So when Loughborough University and the Ministry of Defence announced a strategic collaboration in April 2026 between the UK Supply Chain and Logistics Excellence Centre and the Ministry's own logistics community, aimed at what the Centre's director, Professor Jan Godsell, described as bringing together academic insight and real-world defence expertise to tackle critical end-to-end challenges in UK Defence supply chains, it landed differently for me than it would for most people reading the press release. The Centre's symposium in June 2026 put that relationship in front of an audience: Vice Admiral Andy Kyte, the Ministry of Defence's Chief of Defence Logistics and Support, spoke alongside Professor Godsell on digitalisation and AI across supply chains, at an event hosted by a centre that exists, institutionally, to bring MIT's supply chain curriculum into a UK context.

Whether I am one of the first people in UK defence logistics to have worked through that curriculum independently, ahead of any formal channel into it, I cannot verify and would not want to lean on. Either way, I was reading the syllabus a decade before anyone built a partnership around it, long before there was any institutional reason to.

A New Vocabulary

Eighteen years in Defence logistics teaches you a great deal by feel. You learn roughly how much safety stock to hold before a bad month runs you out, roughly how a small change in demand at one end of a supply chain turns into a large one further down it, roughly which trade-offs a network design is making even when nobody wrote them down. None of it arrives with a name attached, just experience tested against outcomes, refined slowly, and rarely generalising beyond the account or platform where you learned it.

What the MicroMasters gave me instead was a structure for reasoning about that experience, one that generalises past the one account where I originally learned it. Safety stock set against demand variance and a target service level, rather than against habit. The bullwhip effect as a formal explanation for why a small order fluctuation at a retailer arrives as a large one at a manufacturer, rather than something you just learn to expect. Network design as an explicit optimisation between cost and responsiveness, rather than a fixed inheritance from whoever built the chain first. My instinct was already right more often than it was wrong. The coursework explained why, and occasionally caught the cases where it wasn't.

Five courses, one discipline

The programme runs as five courses, and the key concepts document runs to nearly two hundred pages because none of them are light. Supply Chain Analytics opens with probability distributions, hypothesis testing and linear regression, then moves into linear and mixed-integer programming, network algorithms such as Dijkstra's shortest path and the Clark-Wright savings heuristic for vehicle routing, and simulation. Supply Chain Fundamentals covers demand forecasting through exponential smoothing, and inventory theory in real depth: the economic order quantity model and its extensions, stochastic demand, the newsvendor problem, and multi-period and multi-location inventory models. Supply Chain Design applies that machinery to network and facility location models, production planning and material requirements planning, sales and operations planning, procurement, and supply chain finance, the point where a design decision has to be translated into the language a CFO actually uses. Supply Chain Dynamics is the most conceptual of the five: complex systems, the bullwhip effect properly modelled rather than just described, supply chain strategy, global sourcing, and enterprise resilience against exogenous shocks. Supply Chain Technology and Systems closes with relational data models, SQL, an introduction to machine learning, and the enterprise systems, ERP, transportation management, warehouse management, that most of this eventually has to run inside.

That reads more like an engineering curriculum than a survey course, applied to a discipline that most people who practise it for a living have never been asked to think of as one.

Three Flows

There is a definition MIT's own Center for Transportation and Logistics uses that I did not have language for until I met it formally: a supply chain is the physical, financial and information flow between trading partners that ultimately fulfils a customer's request. I had been managing all three flows for most of a career without ever hearing them named as three distinct, interacting things. Ammunition and stores moving is the physical flow. The account reconciliation and the audit trail is the information flow. The budget line it draws against is the financial flow. Every failure I had ever investigated was a breakdown in one flow that the other two did not catch in time, and I had never had a single sentence that said so until the coursework gave me one.

That is really the argument for calling this a discipline rather than a trade: a trade teaches you what to do, and this taught me how to reason about it in a way that would hold up against a problem I hadn't seen before. Eighteen years gave me the first part. Five courses explained it.

The Case for Starting Now

The Ministry of Defence's arrangement with the UK SCALE Centre is, at present, a research partnership rather than a training pipeline, and it would be premature to read a formal career pathway into a base-resilience and digital-manufacturing agenda that does not mention individual qualifications at all. But the two conversations are no longer entirely separate. A MIT-affiliated research centre now sits inside the same defence logistics family whose most senior uniformed logistician is speaking at its symposium, and the self-paced, individually funded coursework behind that centre's parent network has been open to anyone with an internet connection for years, whether or not a formal channel into it yet exists.

For any logistician, serving or civilian, who has done the job long enough to trust their own instinct but has never had the formal language to argue for it outside their own account, I would say the same thing I would have told myself three years ago: the vocabulary is already published, it does not require an institutional pathway to access, and the discipline was there before anyone built a partnership to recognise it.
