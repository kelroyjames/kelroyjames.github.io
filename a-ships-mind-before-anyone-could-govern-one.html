A ship's mind, before anyone could govern one

I went looking for a quote I half-remembered about the Royal Navy wanting artificial intelligence at the heart of a warship. The quote I found was better than the one I remembered, and it belonged to a different admiral than the one I thought said it.

Kelroy James 12 September 2026
📖 6 min read
Share on LinkedIn Share on X Share via Email

Writing my last piece sent me back to a memory I have carried for years, of a First Sea Lord saying he wanted artificial intelligence at the heart of a warship. I went to check it before I used it, the way I now check most memories, and the record corrected me on two counts: I had the wrong admiral, and the wrong year.

Two admirals, two years apart

In September 2015, Admiral Sir George Zambellas, then First Sea Lord, told an audience at the Defence and Security Equipment International exhibition that deploying "intelligent systems at sea, under the sea, in the air and on land" would "revolutionise maritime operations." He named autonomy as one of six technologies set to remake the Royal Navy, alongside additive manufacturing, novel weapons, interoperability, data and people. It is a genuinely early and confident claim, made eleven years ago this month. However, it does not use the words "artificial intelligence," and it does not put anything at the heart of a warship.

That specific claim belongs to his successor. Two years later, in September 2017, Admiral Sir Philip Jones stood at the same exhibition and said it plainly: "Artificial Intelligence is also an important part of this future. Under Project Nelson, the Royal Navy aims to develop a ship's 'mind' at the centre of our warships and headquarters to enable rapid decision making in complex, fast moving operations." A Royal Navy AI system, named, with a purpose and a location, stated by the officer who would have owned the decision to fund it. That is the sentence I had been misremembering as Zambellas's for years, which is itself a small lesson in how a good idea migrates onto whoever you associate most strongly with the era it belongs to.

The trail Project Nelson leaves

Project Nelson had already been reported the previous year. Trade coverage from October 2016 describes the Royal Navy as "keen to explore the potential of using machine-learning to improve operational capability in its fighting units," alongside a related programme called STARTLE, built by Roke Manor Research, which used neural networks to flag threats from sensor data and explicitly logged its own decisions for a human to sign off. That detail is worth sitting with. Explainability and human sign-off were being built into a Royal Navy AI system in 2016, seven years before ISO/IEC 42001 made exactly that pairing, transparency and human oversight, a named requirement for AI management systems generally.

What I could not find is a public verdict on Project Nelson itself. The clearest trace of it I can locate after 2017 is a single sentence in the Royal Navy's own 2022 Digital and Data Plan, citing "the advent of NELSON, Navy X and other initiatives" in a section about attracting digital talent, with no further account of what the programme delivered, how it was assured, or whether the ship's mind Jones described was ever built as described. That silence says less about whether Project Nelson succeeded than about the assurance regime that was supposed to sit around it, which left no visible public record of its own, a different and, in some ways, more interesting problem than an outright failure would be.

Where I actually stood in this

There is a reason I went looking for this quote at all, beyond curiosity. In 2019, while researching my own dissertation on fuel-consumption forecasting, I interviewed engineers working on Project Nelson directly, before I had a name for what I was looking at. I already knew, from those conversations, that the Navy was exploring machine learning for exactly the problem my dissertation examined, which is why it went on to propose using artificial neural networks against the same data. I had already stood next to Project Nelson once, before either the capability or the standard that would eventually govern it had a name I would have recognised.

The gap that opened first

I made an argument in my last piece that Deloitte and Boston Consulting Group are now describing, for AI-enabled enterprises, an assurance destination that military logistics has been operating close to for years. Project Nelson complicates that argument in a useful way, because it shows the same institution reaching for the AI capability years before it had anything resembling a governance standard to point at. Jones asked for a ship's mind in 2017. ISO/IEC 42001, the first international standard for AI management systems, was not published until 2023. Deloitte and BCG's papers making the case for assurance engineered into AI systems by design did not arrive until this September, eleven years after Zambellas's speech and nine after Jones's. The capability ambition and the governance instrument to assure it were never running on the same clock, in defence or, it now appears, anywhere else.

That is a different claim from the one I made last time, and I think it is the more honest one. Naval logistics assurance held up because the discipline underneath it, judgement exercised close to the risk, graded rather than advisory inspection, a mandatory reckoning when ownership changes hands, does not depend on knowing in advance what kind of system it will eventually be asked to assure. Project Nelson's silence in the public record is what happens when the capability arrives and that discipline is not yet attached to it. The Deloitte and BCG papers are, in effect, a proposal to attach it now, at enterprise scale, to a technology that has already been running for years without it.

What I would want to know

I do not know what happened to Project Nelson, and I would rather say that plainly than guess at a tidier ending. What I would want to know, if I could ask either admiral now, is who was accountable for reporting whether the ship's mind was actually built, and whether that accountability survived the change of command between them. That is the question this whole territory keeps returning to, under a different name each time: a record is not reality, a capability announced is not a capability assured, and the distance between the two does not close on its own just because the technology it concerns has changed.
